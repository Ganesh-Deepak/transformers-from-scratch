11 · Decoding and Sampling

The model gives you a probability distribution over 128,000 tokens. Turning that into text is a separate algorithm with its own parameters — and those parameters change model behaviour more than most people realise. A "bad" model is quite often a well-trained model with a badly configured sampler.

11.1 · The generation loop

@torch.no_grad()
def generate(model, ids, max_new=100, **sampler_kwargs):
    for _ in range(max_new):
        logits, _ = model(ids[:, -model.cfg.max_T:])   # (B, T, V)
        next_logits = logits[:, -1, :]                 # (B, V) ← ONLY the last position
        probs = process(next_logits, **sampler_kwargs) # (B, V)
        nxt = torch.multinomial(probs, num_samples=1)  # (B, 1)
        ids = torch.cat([ids, nxt], dim=1)             # (B, T+1)
    return ids
Two things to notice
  1. logits[:, -1, :] — the model computed a prediction for every position, but during generation you only want the last one. The other T−1 predictions are thrown away. (During training you use all of them; that asymmetry is exactly what the causal mask buys you.)
  2. This loop is quadratically wasteful. Every iteration re-runs the whole prefix through the model. That is what the KV cache fixes — Chapter 12.

11.2 · The sampling strategies

Play with this before reading on. Move temperature first, then try top-k and top-p:

Greedy — always take the argmax

nxt = logits.argmax(dim=-1, keepdim=True)

Deterministic and locally optimal. Also, for open-ended text, reliably degenerate: it falls into repetition loops ("I think that I think that I think…"). The reason is subtle and worth knowing — high-probability sequences are not typical sequences. Natural human text is full of moderately surprising choices, and always picking the safest token produces text that is statistically too likely to be human.

Greedy is the right choice for tasks with one correct answer: extraction, classification, structured output, most tool-calling.

Temperature — reshape before sampling

pi = softmax(zi / T) 
probs = (logits / temperature).softmax(dim=-1)
TEffectUse for
→ 0approaches greedy (one spike)deterministic tasks
0.6–0.8focused but not rigidmost assistant use, code
1.0the model's own distribution, untouchedcalibration studies
> 1.2flattened — rare tokens get real probabilitybrainstorming, poetry; degrades fast

Note temperature divides logits, not probabilities. Dividing probabilities and renormalising is a different (and wrong) operation.

Worked example — one distribution, four samplers

The model has produced logits for five candidate next tokens after "the cat sat on the …". Everything below comes from these five numbers.

1

token     mat    floor    rug     sky    idea
logit     3.0     2.0     1.0     0.0    -1.0
p(T=1)  0.636   0.234   0.086   0.032   0.012
A one-unit logit gap is a ~2.7× probability ratio — e¹. Logit differences are multiplicative in probability space.
2
Temperature reshapes before sampling. Same logits, divided by T: 
T=0.5   0.865   0.117   0.016   0.002   0.000    <- sharper, near-greedy
T=1.0   0.636   0.234   0.086   0.032   0.012    <- the model's own belief
T=2.0   0.429   0.260   0.158   0.096   0.058    <- flatter, "idea" now 5%
Note T=2.0 gives "idea" — a nonsense continuation — a 1-in-17 chance. Every high temperature is buying diversity with exactly this kind of risk.
3
Top-k = 2: keep the best two, drop the rest, renormalise. 
keep {mat, floor} -> 0.636, 0.234  ->  renormalised: 0.731, 0.269
"rug" is now impossible, even though the model gave it 8.6%.
4
Top-p = 0.9: keep the smallest set whose mass reaches 0.9. 
cumulative:  0.636   0.871   0.957   0.988   1.000
                              ^ crosses 0.9 here
keeps 3 tokens: {mat, floor, rug}
This is the adaptivity. Here it kept 3. If the model had been certain (p = [0.98, …]) it would keep 1; if genuinely unsure it might keep 200. Top-k cannot do that — k is fixed before the model has spoken.

Read step 4 against step 3. Top-k = 2 and top-p = 0.9 disagree about "rug" on this exact distribution. Neither is more correct; top-p is just letting the model's own confidence decide how many options survive.

import torch
logits = torch.tensor([3., 2., 1., 0., -1.])          # mat floor rug sky idea

for T in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
    print(T, [round(v, 3) for v in (logits / T).softmax(-1).tolist()])

p = logits.softmax(-1)
print("cumulative:", [round(v, 3) for v in p.cumsum(0).tolist()])
print("top-p 0.9 keeps:", int(((p.cumsum(0) - p) < 0.9).sum()), "tokens")   # 3
print("top-k 2:", [round(v, 3) for v in (p[:2] / p[:2].sum()).tolist()])    # 0.731, 0.269
Sanity check Softmax output must sum to 1 before truncation and again after renormalising. If a sampler ever leaves you with a row that doesn't, it is dropping probability mass.

Top-k — keep the k best

v, ix = logits.topk(k, dim=-1)
logits = torch.full_like(logits, float('-inf')).scatter_(-1, ix, v)
probs = logits.softmax(dim=-1)

Simple, but the fixed k is the flaw. When the model is confident (after "the capital of France is"), k=50 lets in 49 nonsense options. When it's genuinely uncertain (start of a story), k=50 cuts off legitimate continuations.

Top-p / nucleus — keep the smallest set summing to p

sorted_logits, sorted_ix = logits.sort(descending=True, dim=-1)
cum = sorted_logits.softmax(dim=-1).cumsum(dim=-1)
remove = cum - sorted_logits.softmax(dim=-1) > p       # keep the token that crosses p
remove_orig = remove.scatter(-1, sorted_ix, remove)
logits = logits.masked_fill(remove_orig, float('-inf'))

Adaptive, which is why it won. Confident step → the nucleus might be 1 token. Uncertain step → it might be 200. p = 0.9–0.95 is the common default, and it's the single most important sampler knob after temperature.

Min-p — the newer alternative

threshold = min_p * probs.max(dim=-1, keepdim=True).values
probs = probs.masked_fill(probs < threshold, 0)

Keep tokens with probability at least min_p × (the top token's probability). Scales the cutoff with model confidence directly rather than through a cumulative sum. It's noticeably more robust than top-p at high temperature, and has been adopted across llama.cpp, vLLM and HuggingFace.

Repetition controls

# Penalise tokens already present (applied to logits, before softmax)
for tok in set(generated_ids):
    logits[tok] /= penalty if logits[tok] > 0 else 1/penalty   # penalty ≈ 1.1

# Or: frequency / presence penalties (OpenAI-style, additive)
logits[tok] -= frequency_penalty * count[tok] + presence_penalty * (count[tok] > 0)
Use repetition penalties sparingly

They are blunt instruments. A penalty of 1.2 will suppress "the" and "(" just as hard as it suppresses a looping phrase, and in code generation it will actively break syntax by discouraging required repeated tokens. If a model loops badly, the usual real fixes are a better sampler configuration or a better model — not a heavier penalty.

11.3 · Beam search, and why LLMs don't use it

Beam search keeps the k highest-probability sequences rather than tokens, expanding all of them each step. It's the right algorithm when there is a single correct output and you want to maximise sequence likelihood.

Beam searchSampling
Optimisestotal sequence probabilitydraws from the distribution
Output diversityvery lowtunable
Costk× the compute and k× the KV cache1×
Good fortranslation, ASR, constrained transductionopen-ended generation

For open-ended text, higher sequence probability actively makes output worse — the same "likely ≠ typical" problem as greedy, amplified. Holtzman et al.'s "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" (2019) demonstrated this and introduced nucleus sampling as the fix. Beam search is still standard in Whisper and translation models, where there genuinely is a right answer.

Why does top-p generally beat top-k?
It's faster to compute
It never truncates the distribution
The number of candidates adapts to the model's confidence at each step
It works better with high temperature
Adaptivity. A fixed k is wrong in both directions — too permissive when the model is certain, too restrictive when it isn't. Top-p keeps a variable-sized set defined by probability mass. (It's actually slightly slower — it needs a sort.)
You set temperature=0 and top_p=0.9. What happens?
Greedy decoding — temperature 0 collapses to argmax and top-p becomes irrelevant
A division-by-zero error
The nucleus is applied first, then greedy within it — same as top-p alone
Random sampling from the top 90%
Greedy. Most implementations special-case T=0 to argmax (rather than actually dividing by zero). The top token has probability ~1.0, so the 0.9 nucleus contains exactly one token anyway. Setting both is a very common source of confusion — people tune top_p and see no effect because temperature 0 already decided everything.
A code-generation model keeps producing repeated lines. Best first intervention?
Set repetition_penalty=1.5
Check the prompt format and chat template, then try modest temperature (0.2–0.6) with top-p
Increase temperature to 1.5
Switch to beam search
Check the format first. Looping in an instruct model is very often a chat-template mismatch (Ch 3) rather than a sampler problem. A heavy repetition penalty is actively harmful for code — it discourages the repeated , ), and keyword tokens that valid syntax requires. High temperature makes code worse, and beam search makes it repetitive.

11.4 · Constrained decoding

If you need output to be valid JSON or match a grammar, don't ask nicely — make invalid tokens impossible:

# At each step, a state machine tells you which tokens are legal.
allowed = grammar.allowed_token_ids(state)      # e.g. after '{' only '"' or '}' is valid
logits[~allowed] = float('-inf')

This gives a 100% guarantee of syntactic validity — you cannot generate malformed JSON because malformed tokens have zero probability. Implementations: outlines, guidance, llama.cpp's GBNF grammars, vLLM's structured-output backends, and the OpenAI/Anthropic structured-output APIs.

Constrained ≠ correct

You are guaranteed valid syntax, not valid content. Forcing a schema can also hurt quality: if the model wants to reason before answering and the grammar forbids it, you've removed its working space. The usual fix is a two-stage approach — free-form reasoning first, then a constrained extraction pass.

Note on reasoning models

Models trained with RL on verifiable rewards (Chapter 21) frequently have publisher-specified sampling parameters, and deviating measurably hurts benchmark scores — greedy decoding in particular can cause repetition loops in some of them. This is a real behavioural difference from base/instruct models. Always read the model card's recommended settings before benchmarking anything.

11.6 · Exercises

11.1Implement every samplercode

Write sample(logits, temperature, top_k, top_p, min_p) handling all combinations, in the correct order: temperature → top-k → top-p → min-p → renormalise → sample. Test that temperature=0 gives argmax and that each filter reduces the support as expected.

Solution
def sample(logits, temperature=1.0, top_k=0, top_p=1.0, min_p=0.0):
    if temperature == 0:
        return logits.argmax(-1, keepdim=True)

    logits = logits / temperature

    if top_k > 0:
        kth = logits.topk(min(top_k, logits.size(-1)), dim=-1).values[..., -1:]
        logits = logits.masked_fill(logits < kth, float('-inf'))

    if top_p < 1.0:
        sl, si = logits.sort(descending=True, dim=-1)
        cum = sl.softmax(-1).cumsum(-1)
        drop = cum - sl.softmax(-1) > top_p        # keep the crossing token
        logits = logits.masked_fill(drop.scatter(-1, si, drop), float('-inf'))

    probs = logits.softmax(-1)

    if min_p > 0:
        probs = probs.masked_fill(probs < min_p * probs.amax(-1, keepdim=True), 0)
        probs = probs / probs.sum(-1, keepdim=True)

    return torch.multinomial(probs, 1)
11.2Map the temperature–quality curveexploration

With your Chapter 10 Shakespeare model, generate 500 characters at T ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.3, 2.0}. For each, measure: distinct-token ratio (unique/total), longest repeated substring, and your own 1–5 readability score.

What you'll see

T=0.1 produces loops and a low distinct ratio. T=2.0 produces high diversity and non-words. The readability peak is usually 0.7–0.9, and — importantly — the diversity and quality metrics peak at different temperatures. There is no single best T; there's a trade-off curve, and where you sit on it should be a product decision.

Using GPT-2, generate 200 tokens from the same prompt with (a) greedy and (b) top-p 0.95. Then compute, for both outputs and for a real human paragraph, the average −log p the model assigns to each token.

The result, and why it matters

Greedy text has much lower average surprisal than human text. Nucleus text sits close to the human value. This is Holtzman et al.'s central figure: human language is not the most probable language. Maximising likelihood at generation time moves you away from the distribution you trained to match — a genuinely counterintuitive result, and the reason nobody uses greedy decoding for prose.
