18 · Scaling Laws and Long Context

Two questions that determine how models get built: given a compute budget, how big should the model be? and how do you make a model trained at 4k tokens work at 128k? Both have surprisingly clean quantitative answers.

18.1 · Scaling laws

Kaplan et al. (2020) found something remarkable: test loss follows a smooth power law in model size, dataset size, and compute — over seven orders of magnitude, with no visible discontinuity.

L(N, D) = E + ANα + BDβ Hoffmann et al. 2022 fit: E = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28

Read the three terms:

  • E — the irreducible entropy of language. You cannot beat this no matter what.
  • A/Nα — the penalty for having too few parameters.
  • B/Dβ — the penalty for having seen too little data.

The Chinchilla correction

Kaplan's original analysis concluded you should scale model size much faster than data. GPT-3 followed that advice: 175B parameters on 300B tokens — about 1.7 tokens per parameter.

Hoffmann et al. (2022) redid the experiments with proper learning-rate schedules for each budget and found the opposite: N and D should scale roughly equally, at about 20 tokens per parameter. They proved it by training Chinchilla (70B on 1.4T tokens), which beat Gopher (280B on 300B tokens) across the board at the same training compute — a 4× smaller model, decisively better.

The three-line derivation

With C ≈ 6ND fixed, substitute D = C/(6N) into the loss and minimise over N. Because α ≈ β, the optimum has N* ∝ C^0.5 and D* ∝ C^0.5 — both scale as the square root of compute, which is why the ratio D/N stays roughly constant at ~20. Every 4× of compute should buy you 2× the parameters and 2× the data.

Worked example — was GPT-3 the right size? (the fitted law says no)

GPT-3 used C ≈ 3.14 × 10²³ FLOPs on 175 B parameters and 300 B tokens. Plug its budget into the Hoffmann fit and ask what it should have been.

1
The compute-optimal split at that budget. With C = 6ND and D = 20N: N* = √(C / 120). 
N* = sqrt(3.14e23 / 120) = 51.2 B parameters
D* = 3.14e23 / (6 × 51.2e9) = 1,023 B tokens
2
Predicted loss, both ways. 
GPT-3 as built   N=175B  D=300B   ratio 1.7 tok/param   loss 2.0023
compute-optimal  N=51B   D=1023B  ratio 20  tok/param   loss 1.9610
A model 3.4× smaller, trained on 3.4× more data for the same compute, was predicted to be better. That is the Chinchilla result in one line — and it is why the field pivoted so hard in 2022.
3
The optimum is a ridge, not a peak. Same formula across budgets: 
C=1e21   N*=  2.9B   D*=   58B   loss 2.335
C=1e22   N*=  9.1B   D*=  183B   loss 2.146

C=1e25   N*=289B     D*= 5774B   loss 1.851
Every 10× of compute buys ~3.2× the parameters and ~3.2× the data — both scale as √C, which is why the ratio stays pinned at 20.
4
And why nobody obeys it. Llama-3-8B used 1875 tokens per parameter — 94× past "optimal": 
Llama-3-8B   N=8B  D=15T   loss 1.9485
Better than GPT-3's predicted 2.0023, with 22× fewer parameters. It cost far more to train per unit of quality — and it is far cheaper to serve, forever.

Read steps 2 and 4 together. Chinchilla answers "best loss for a fixed training budget". Llama-3 answers "best model I can afford to run". Both are correct; they optimise different things, and the second one is what ships.

import math
E, A, B_, a, b = 1.69, 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28          # Hoffmann et al. 2022
def loss(N, D): return E + A / N**a + B_ / D**b

C = 3.14e23                                              # GPT-3's budget
N_opt = math.sqrt(C / 120); D_opt = C / (6 * N_opt)
print(round(loss(175e9, 300e9), 4))    # 2.0023  as built
print(round(loss(N_opt, D_opt), 4))    # 1.9610  compute-optimal
print(round(loss(8e9, 15e12), 4))      # 1.9485  Llama-3-8B
assert loss(N_opt, D_opt) < loss(175e9, 300e9)
Caveat These are fitted predictions, not measurements of those models. The constants come from Hoffmann et al.'s sweep on their data mixture; a different corpus moves them. The direction of the conclusion is robust, the third decimal place is not.

Why nobody actually trains Chinchilla-optimal any more

Chinchilla optimises training compute. But a deployed model's compute is dominated by inference, paid on every request forever. A smaller model that's slightly worse but 3× cheaper to serve is often the better product.

Meta's own comment on Llama 3 was that 8B models continued improving log-linearly well past 15T tokens — far beyond where Chinchilla says to stop. The loss surface near the optimum is flat, so you can move a long way along the "smaller model, more data" direction for very little loss penalty, and buy a large inference saving. Drag the widget's parameter slider away from optimal and watch how little the loss moves.

Scaling laws describe loss, not capability

Loss is smooth. Benchmark scores sometimes are not — they can look like they jump at a threshold. Wei et al. (2022) called these "emergent abilities"; Schaeffer et al. (2023) argued persuasively that the jumps are largely an artifact of discontinuous metrics (exact-match scoring turns smooth improvement into a step). Use smooth metrics before claiming emergence. The underlying loss almost never jumps.

~29B / ~580B. From C = 6ND and D = 20N: 10²³ = 120N² → N = √(8.3×10²⁰) ≈ 2.9×10¹⁰. Then D = 20N ≈ 5.8×10¹¹. Note options B and D aren't wrong as engineering choices — they're deliberately over-trained, which is what you'd actually ship. They're just not compute-optimal.

Total cost of ownership. Chinchilla answers "best loss for a fixed training budget". Meta's question was "best model I can serve cheaply forever". Since the loss surface is flat near the optimum, moving far down the over-training direction costs little loss and saves enormously at inference. Chinchilla isn't wrong — it answers a different question.

18.2 · Long context

A model trained at 4k tokens degrades badly at 32k (Chapter 7). RoPE's rotation angles go far outside the range it saw. Three families of fix:

Position Interpolation (PI)

# Squash positions into the trained range instead of extrapolating past it.
scale = trained_len / target_len          # e.g. 4096/32768 = 1/8
positions = torch.arange(T) * scale       # position 32767 → "4095.9"

Chen et al. (2023). Simple, works, needs a little fine-tuning. The cost: it compresses all frequencies equally, which crushes the high-frequency dimensions that encode fine local ordering — the model gets worse at distinguishing adjacent tokens.

NTK-aware scaling

# Instead of scaling positions, scale the RoPE BASE.
base_new = base * scale ** (d / (d - 2))     # e.g. 10000 → ~much larger

Interpolate the low-frequency (long-range) dimensions while leaving high-frequency ones nearly untouched. Better than PI, and often works with no fine-tuning at all. Llama 3's jump from base 10,000 to 500,000 is a training-time application of the same insight.

YaRN

Peng et al. (2023) — the current best of the three. It applies a per-dimension strategy based on each dimension's wavelength relative to the context:

  • Wavelength ≪ context → the dimension has seen full rotations; don't touch it (extrapolate).
  • Wavelength ≫ context → never completed a rotation; interpolate.
  • In between → blend smoothly.

Plus a temperature adjustment on the attention logits to compensate for the entropy change from longer sequences. YaRN reaches long contexts with roughly 10× less fine-tuning data than PI, and is what most context-extension work uses today.

Sliding-window attention

# Each token attends only to the previous W tokens.
mask = (i - j < W) & (j <= i)          # W = 4096 typically

Turns O(T²) into O(T·W). Information still propagates further than W because stacking layers extends the receptive field — n_layer × W in total, much like a CNN. Mistral 7B popularised this; Gemma 2/3 interleave local and global layers (e.g. 5 local : 1 global) so most layers are cheap while a few retain full-range attention.

Attention sinks — why naive window eviction breaks models

Chapter 5's exercise found that many heads dump attention on token 0 regardless of content. Softmax forces every row to sum to 1, so a head with nothing relevant to attend to needs somewhere to put its mass — the first token becomes a "null" option. Evict token 0 from the cache and quality collapses. StreamingLLM's fix (Xiao et al. 2023) is trivial and effective: always keep the first ~4 tokens plus the sliding window. That one change enables stable generation over millions of tokens.

18.3 · Measuring long context honestly

"Supports 1M tokens" is a claim about the config file

A model's advertised context length says what it will accept without erroring — not what it can use. Effective context is routinely a fraction of advertised context.

BenchmarkWhat it measuresDifficulty
Needle in a Haystackretrieve one planted fact from a long documenteasy — most models pass; near-saturated
RULERmulti-needle, aggregation, variable tracking, multi-hop QAhard — effective lengths often far below advertised
LongBench / ∞Benchrealistic long-document tasksrealistic
"Lost in the middle"accuracy vs. position of the relevant factreveals a U-shaped curve: strong at the ends, weak in the middle

The last one is the most practically useful finding: information at the beginning and end of a long context is used far more reliably than information in the middle. If you're building a RAG system, put the most important retrieved chunks at the edges — it measurably improves answers.

Why does YaRN outperform uniform position interpolation?
It treats each RoPE dimension differently based on whether its wavelength is shorter or longer than the context
It uses a larger model
It doesn't need any fine-tuning
It removes RoPE entirely
Per-dimension treatment. Uniform PI compresses high-frequency dimensions that were already working fine, damaging local resolution. YaRN leaves those alone and only interpolates the low-frequency dimensions that genuinely never completed a rotation in training. (It still benefits from some fine-tuning — just far less.)
You implement sliding-window attention and evict old KV entries. Quality collapses after the window fills. Most likely cause?
The window is too small
RoPE needs rescaling
You evicted the attention-sink tokens at the start of the sequence
The KV cache is corrupted
Attention sinks. This is the exact failure StreamingLLM diagnosed. Many heads route surplus attention mass to the first few tokens; remove them and every softmax row is forced to redistribute that mass onto tokens it didn't want, wrecking the output. Keep the first ~4 tokens permanently and the problem disappears.

18.4 · Exercises

18.1Fit your own scaling lawcode

Train tiny models at 5 sizes (roughly 0.1M, 0.3M, 1M, 3M, 10M non-embedding params) on Tiny Shakespeare for a fixed number of tokens each. Plot final loss vs N on log-log axes and fit a power law.

  1. What exponent do you get? Is it near 0.34?
  2. Extrapolate to 100M. Then train a 100M model and check.
What to expect

You'll get a clean power law over the small range, but the exponent will likely differ from 0.34 — Chinchilla's constants are fitted on a specific data distribution at much larger scale. The form generalises; the constants don't. Your extrapolation will also break down as you approach the dataset's irreducible entropy, since 1 MB of Shakespeare is exhausted long before 100M parameters are.

18.2Extend a model's contexthard

Take your Chapter 11 model trained at max_T=128. Evaluate perplexity at sequence lengths 128, 256, 512, 1024 with: (a) no change, (b) position interpolation, (c) NTK-aware base scaling, (d) YaRN. Plot perplexity vs length for all four.

Expected shape

(a) explodes immediately past 128. (b) degrades gracefully but is worse than (a) at 128 — the price of compressing frequencies you didn't need to compress. (c) and (d) stay flat furthest, with YaRN best at the longest lengths. That "PI is worse at the original length" crossover is the key observation and the reason the field moved past PI.

18.3Run your own needle testapplied

Build a needle-in-a-haystack harness: insert "The secret code is 7391." at depth d ∈ {0%, 10%, …, 100%} into filler text of length L ∈ {1k, 2k, 4k, 8k, 16k}, then ask the model for the code. Build a heatmap of accuracy over (L, d) for any local model.

What you'll find

A characteristic pattern: strong at depth 0% and 100%, weakest around 30–70% — the "lost in the middle" effect — with the weak band widening as L grows. Then try the harder version: two needles that must be combined. Accuracy usually falls off a cliff. Single-needle retrieval is a much easier task than reasoning over long context, which is why RULER exists and why NIAH scores are a poor basis for choosing a model.
