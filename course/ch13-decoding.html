13 · Decoding and Sampling

The model gives you a probability distribution over 128,000 tokens. Turning that into text is a separate algorithm with its own parameters — and those parameters change model behaviour more than most people realise. A "bad" model is quite often a well-trained model with a badly configured sampler.

Builds onCh 09 — logits come out of the forward pass
Leads toCh 15 — this loop is quadratically wasteful — the cache fixes it
Ch 18 — speculative decoding rewrites it again
Ch 25 — sampling many answers is how RL on reasoning learns
Why this chapterThe model gives a distribution. Turning it into text is a separate algorithm with its own bugs.
The whole chapter in five sentences

A sampler turns one logit vector into one token, and its settings (temperature, top-p, min-p) change behaviour more than most model changes do. Constraints are masks: a grammar engine walks each token's characters through an automaton and sets the illegal ones to −∞, and the hard part is that grammar boundaries and token boundaries do not line up. More samples buy accuracy when you can verify or vote, but voting only amplifies what the model already believes most. Stopping is a streaming problem, because stop strings span tokens. Temperature 0 is not reproducible on a server, because batch size changes the order of floating-point additions.

13.1 · The generation loop

@torch.no_grad()
def generate(model, ids, max_new=100, **sampler_kwargs):
    for _ in range(max_new):
        logits, _ = model(ids[:, -model.cfg.max_T:])   # (B, T, V)
        next_logits = logits[:, -1, :]                 # (B, V) ← ONLY the last position
        probs = process(next_logits, **sampler_kwargs) # (B, V)
        nxt = torch.multinomial(probs, num_samples=1)  # (B, 1)
        ids = torch.cat([ids, nxt], dim=1)             # (B, T+1)
    return ids
Two things to notice
  1. logits[:, -1, :] — the model computed a prediction for every position, but during generation you only want the last one. The other T−1 predictions are thrown away. (During training you use all of them; that asymmetry is exactly what the causal mask buys you.)
  2. This loop is quadratically wasteful. Every iteration re-runs the whole prefix through the model. That is what the KV cache fixes — Chapter 15.

13.2 · The sampling strategies

Play with this before reading on. Move temperature first, then try top-k and top-p:

Greedy — always take the argmax

nxt = logits.argmax(dim=-1, keepdim=True)

Deterministic and locally optimal. Also, for open-ended text, reliably degenerate: it falls into repetition loops ("I think that I think that I think…"). The reason is subtle and worth knowing — high-probability sequences are not typical sequences. Natural human text is full of moderately surprising choices, and always picking the safest token produces text that is statistically too likely to be human.

Greedy is the right choice for tasks with one correct answer: extraction, classification, structured output, most tool-calling.

Temperature — reshape before sampling

pi = softmax(zi / T) 
probs = (logits / temperature).softmax(dim=-1)
TEffectUse for
→ 0approaches greedy (one spike)deterministic tasks
0.6–0.8focused but not rigidmost assistant use, code
1.0the model's own distribution, untouchedcalibration studies
> 1.2flattened — rare tokens get real probabilitybrainstorming, poetry; degrades fast

Note temperature divides logits, not probabilities. Dividing probabilities and renormalising is a different (and wrong) operation.

Read step 4 against step 3. Top-k = 2 and top-p = 0.9 disagree about "rug" on this exact distribution. Neither is more correct; top-p is just letting the model's own confidence decide how many options survive.

import torch
logits = torch.tensor([3., 2., 1., 0., -1.])          # mat floor rug sky idea

for T in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
    print(T, [round(v, 3) for v in (logits / T).softmax(-1).tolist()])

p = logits.softmax(-1)
print("cumulative:", [round(v, 3) for v in p.cumsum(0).tolist()])
print("top-p 0.9 keeps:", int(((p.cumsum(0) - p) < 0.9).sum()), "tokens")   # 3
print("top-k 2:", [round(v, 3) for v in (p[:2] / p[:2].sum()).tolist()])    # 0.731, 0.269
Sanity check Softmax output must sum to 1 before truncation and again after renormalising. If a sampler ever leaves you with a row that doesn't, it is dropping probability mass.

Top-k — keep the k best

v, ix = logits.topk(k, dim=-1)
logits = torch.full_like(logits, float('-inf')).scatter_(-1, ix, v)
probs = logits.softmax(dim=-1)

Simple, but the fixed k is the flaw. When the model is confident (after "the capital of France is"), k=50 lets in 49 nonsense options. When it's genuinely uncertain (start of a story), k=50 cuts off legitimate continuations.

Top-p / nucleus — keep the smallest set summing to p

sorted_logits, sorted_ix = logits.sort(descending=True, dim=-1)
cum = sorted_logits.softmax(dim=-1).cumsum(dim=-1)
remove = cum - sorted_logits.softmax(dim=-1) > p       # keep the token that crosses p
remove_orig = remove.scatter(-1, sorted_ix, remove)
logits = logits.masked_fill(remove_orig, float('-inf'))

Adaptive, which is why it won. Confident step → the nucleus might be 1 token. Uncertain step → it might be 200. p = 0.9–0.95 is the common default, and it's the single most important sampler knob after temperature.

Min-p — the newer alternative

threshold = min_p * probs.max(dim=-1, keepdim=True).values
probs = probs.masked_fill(probs < threshold, 0)

Keep tokens with probability at least min_p × (the top token's probability). Scales the cutoff with model confidence directly rather than through a cumulative sum. It's noticeably more robust than top-p at high temperature, and has been adopted across llama.cpp, vLLM and HuggingFace.

Repetition controls

# Penalise tokens already present (applied to logits, before softmax)
for tok in set(generated_ids):
    logits[tok] /= penalty if logits[tok] > 0 else 1/penalty   # penalty ≈ 1.1

# Or: frequency / presence penalties (OpenAI-style, additive)
logits[tok] -= frequency_penalty * count[tok] + presence_penalty * (count[tok] > 0)
Use repetition penalties sparingly

They are blunt instruments. A penalty of 1.2 will suppress "the" and "(" just as hard as it suppresses a looping phrase, and in code generation it will actively break syntax by discouraging required repeated tokens. If a model loops badly, the usual real fixes are a better sampler configuration or a better model — not a heavier penalty.

13.3 · Beam search, and why LLMs don't use it

Beam search keeps the k highest-probability sequences rather than tokens, expanding all of them each step. It's the right algorithm when there is a single correct output and you want to maximise sequence likelihood.

For open-ended text, higher sequence probability actively makes output worse — the same "likely ≠ typical" problem as greedy, amplified. Holtzman et al.'s "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" (2019) demonstrated this and introduced nucleus sampling as the fix. Beam search is still standard in Whisper and translation models, where there genuinely is a right answer.

13.4 · Constrained decoding

If you need output to be valid JSON or match a grammar, don't ask nicely — make invalid tokens impossible:

# At each step, a state machine tells you which tokens are legal.
allowed = grammar.allowed_token_ids(state)      # e.g. after '{' only '"' or '}' is valid
logits[~allowed] = float('-inf')

This gives a 100% guarantee of syntactic validity: malformed tokens have zero probability, so you cannot sample them. Implementations: outlines, guidance / llguidance, llama.cpp's GBNF grammars, XGrammar, the structured-output backends of vLLM and SGLang, and the structured-output modes of the hosted APIs. The interesting part is what allowed_token_ids has to do, because the grammar is written in characters and the model speaks in tokens.

From a character automaton to a token mask

A grammar engine compiles the grammar into an automaton over characters (bytes, really): a finite-state machine for a regular expression, a pushdown automaton (an FSM with a stack, for nesting) for a context-free grammar such as JSON. A token is allowed in state s if feeding every character of the token, one after another, keeps the automaton alive. That is the whole rule, and tfs/decoding.py implements it in a dozen lines for a toy grammar.

Worked example — masking a 22-token vocabulary against {"n": <digits>}

The grammar is the literal {"n": , then one or more digits, then }. The toy vocabulary has 22 entries, including multi-character ones such as {", n": and 7}, exactly the kind of merges a real BPE vocabulary is full of.

1
At the start, only tokens whose characters are a prefix of {"n": survive: 
allowed: '{'  '{"'                         2 of 22 tokens
2
After {"n": 1, the automaton is inside the digit run. A digit continues it; a } ends it and closes the object: 
allowed: '1' '2' '7' '12' '42' '7}' '}'      7 of 22 tokens
Note 7}: one token that finishes the number and closes the object. It crosses a grammar boundary.
3
The naive engine gets this wrong. One that matches tokens against one grammar terminal at a time (a token must lie inside the current terminal, here "the number") allows only '1' '2' '7' '12' '42'. It bans 7}, and in this toy version even a bare }, which it would only accept after the number had been closed some other way. Banning boundary-crossing tokens does not break validity, but it forces the model onto spellings it rarely saw in training.
4
Which spelling would the model have produced? The tokenizer's own split of {"n": 17} (longest match first here) is 
'{"'  'n"'  ':'  ' '  '1'  '7}'               6 tokens
yet 24 different token sequences spell the same 9 characters with this vocabulary. Every one is valid under the grammar, and only one of them is what the model was trained to see. Steering it onto one of the other 23 (say '{' '"' 'n' '"' ...) puts it slightly off-distribution at every step.

The lesson. Validity is a property of characters; quality depends on the token path. A good engine checks tokens character by character (so boundary-crossing tokens survive) and lets the model pick the path. All five numbers above are asserted in test_grammar_masks.

The token-boundary problem, from the prompt side

The same mismatch bites where the prompt ends. If a prompt ends in "name": " and the tokenizer would normally have merged that final quote with the first characters of the value, the model now sees an unusual split and its next-token distribution is distorted: the trailing-space effect from Chapter 3, in another form. Token healing (introduced in Microsoft's guidance) backs up one token, removes it from the prompt, and constrains the first generated token to start with the removed characters, so the model can choose the merge it would have chosen. Grammar engines meet the same issue in the middle of generation, which is why they track a partial token's characters rather than only whole tokens.

Making it fast: XGrammar

The naive engine is expensive. Walking 128,000 tokens through the automaton every step costs far more than the GPU's forward pass for a small model, and it runs on the CPU, so the GPU waits. XGrammar (Dong et al., 2024) splits the vocabulary at each automaton position into two kinds:

  • Context-independent tokens, whose validity depends only on the current automaton position, not on the stack below it. Their masks are precomputed once per grammar and cached.
  • Context-dependent tokens, which could pop the stack (a } closing an object whose opening depends on the history). Only these are checked at run time, and they are a small minority.

It also overlaps mask computation on the CPU with the forward pass on the GPU. The paper reports up to 100× faster mask generation than earlier engines and up to 80× end-to-end speedup for structured-output serving of Llama-3.1 on an H100 (the authors' measurements). Chapter 18 returns to what this means for a serving stack.

Constrained ≠ correct

You are guaranteed valid syntax, not valid content. Forcing a schema can also hurt quality: if the model wants to reason before answering and the grammar forbids it, you've removed its working space. The usual fix is a two-stage approach (free-form reasoning first, then a constrained extraction pass), or a schema with a free-text reasoning field placed before the answer fields, since the model generates fields in order.

13.5 · Spending more samples: best-of-n and self-consistency

Everything so far draws one sample. For tasks with a checkable or votable answer, drawing several and choosing among them is often the cheapest accuracy you can buy, and it is the simplest form of test-time compute. Two ways to choose:

  • Best-of-n with a verifier. Sample n answers, score each with something that can tell right from wrong (unit tests, a checker, a reward model), keep the best. If the verifier is perfect and each sample is right with probability p, you succeed when any sample is right.
  • Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022). No verifier: sample n reasoning paths, extract each final answer, return the most common one. It works when the right answer is the model's most likely answer even though it is far from certain.
P(at least one right in n) = 1 − (1 − p)n the ceiling for best-of-n with a perfect verifier · Chen et al. (2021) give an unbiased estimator, pass@k = 1 − C(n−c, k) / C(n, k), from n samples with c correct

What it costs. n samples are n decodes, but not n prefills: the prompt is processed once and its KV cache shared by all n sequences (copy-on-write blocks, Chapter 18), so best-of-8 on a long prompt costs far less than 8 separate requests. Samples must be diverse to help, which means temperature well above 0; eight greedy samples are one sample eight times.

13.6 · Stopping: EOS, stop sequences, and streaming

Generation ends in one of three ways: the model emits its end token (<|eot_id|> for Llama 3, a different id for every family, and a wrong one in your config means generation never stops), the length limit is reached, or the text matches a stop sequence the caller supplied, such as "\n\nUser:". The first two are a comparison on token ids. The third is harder than it looks, because stop sequences are strings and they almost never line up with token boundaries.

Worked example — a stop string that arrives in three pieces

Stop sequence "\n\nUser:". The model emits tokens whose text is "Sure", ".\n", "\nUs", "er", ": hi". We stream to the client as we go.

1
"Sure" cannot begin the stop string: send it.
2
".\n": the trailing \n could be the start of \n\nUser:. Send ".", hold back "\n".
3
"\nUs", "er": the held-back text grows to "\n\nUser", still a prefix of the stop string. Send nothing.
4
": hi" completes the stop string. Stop. The client received exactly "Sure.", and never saw a fragment of the stop string it would have had to retract. 
emitted per token:  'Sure'  '.'  ''  ''  ''      -> "Sure."   stopped

Hold-back rule: after each token, keep back the longest suffix of the pending text that is a proper prefix of any stop string; release the rest. StopStreamer in tfs/decoding.py is 30 lines; test_stop_sequences_streaming checks this trace and that held-back text is released, not lost, if the stream ends without a stop.

Three stop-sequence bugs that ship
  1. Matching per token. Checking each token's text for the stop string misses every stop that spans tokens, which is most of them.
  2. Streaming before checking. Sending tokens immediately and stopping later leaks the start of the stop string ("\n\nUs") to the client.
  3. Bytes, not characters. A token can end in the middle of a multi-byte UTF-8 character (Chapter 3). Detokenize with a byte buffer and hold back incomplete characters, or the client receives �.

13.7 · Why temperature 0 is not reproducible

Set temperature to 0, send the same prompt to an inference server twice, and you will sometimes get different completions. The usual explanation ("GPUs are nondeterministic, threads race") is mostly wrong for inference: the forward pass of a typical engine does not use atomic adds, and the same kernel on the same input gives the same bits every time. He and Thinking Machines Lab (2025) traced the real cause: the kernels are not batch-invariant. The result for your request depends on how many other requests the server happened to batch with it.

The chain is short. Floating-point addition is not associative, so a sum's last bits depend on the order of additions. To keep the GPU busy, matmul and attention kernels change their reduction strategy with problem size, for instance splitting a long dot product into more chunks (split-K, Chapter 17) when the batch is small. Batch size changes the order; the order changes the bits; and when two candidate tokens are nearly tied, the bits change the argmax.

Worked example — the same numbers, summed six ways
1
Order matters. In fp32, 1e8 + 1 = 1e8 (the spacing between fp32 numbers near 108 is 8, Chapter 0). So: 
(1e8 + 1) + (-1e8)  =  0
(1e8 + (-1e8)) + 1  =  1
2
Chunking changes the answer. 4096 random fp32 values, summed the way a split-K kernel does (each chunk left to right, then the partial sums), every addition rounded to fp32: 
chunks:    1           2           4           8           16          32
sum:    27.716221   27.716259   27.716211   27.716192   27.716166   27.716141
exact (fp64 fsum):  27.716160
Six chunkings, six different fp32 results, all within 1.2 × 10−4 of each other.
3
A flipped argmax. Two candidate tokens whose exact logits differ by about 1.07 × 10−6 (26.6436109 vs 26.6436099; token 0 should win). Computing both 1024-long dot products with 1 chunk and then with 8: 
split 1:   token 0 = 26.643583   token 1 = 26.643604   -> greedy picks token 1
split 8:   token 0 = 26.643608   token 1 = 26.643597   -> greedy picks token 0
One flipped token early in a completion and every token after it can differ.

The sums are done one fp32 addition at a time in pure Python so they reproduce bit for bit on any machine: sum_fp32 and find_argmax_flip, asserted in test_fp32_reduction_order and test_argmax_flip.

What to do about it. For evaluation, do not assume a single temperature-0 run is "the" answer: near-ties are common in long outputs. For RL on a model's own samples (Chapter 25), a mismatch between the sampler's numbers and the trainer's numbers is a source of off-policy error, which is the case He et al. make for batch-invariant kernels: kernels that fix one reduction order regardless of batch size, at some cost in speed. Serving engines have begun to offer deterministic or batch-invariant modes for exactly this use.

13.9 · Exercises

13.1Implement every samplercode

Write sample(logits, temperature, top_k, top_p, min_p) handling all combinations, in the correct order: temperature → top-k → top-p → min-p → renormalise → sample. Test that temperature=0 gives argmax and that each filter reduces the support as expected.

Solution
def sample(logits, temperature=1.0, top_k=0, top_p=1.0, min_p=0.0):
    if temperature == 0:
        return logits.argmax(-1, keepdim=True)

    logits = logits / temperature

    if top_k > 0:
        kth = logits.topk(min(top_k, logits.size(-1)), dim=-1).values[..., -1:]
        logits = logits.masked_fill(logits < kth, float('-inf'))

    if top_p < 1.0:
        sl, si = logits.sort(descending=True, dim=-1)
        cum = sl.softmax(-1).cumsum(-1)
        drop = cum - sl.softmax(-1) > top_p        # keep the crossing token
        logits = logits.masked_fill(drop.scatter(-1, si, drop), float('-inf'))

    probs = logits.softmax(-1)

    if min_p > 0:
        probs = probs.masked_fill(probs < min_p * probs.amax(-1, keepdim=True), 0)
        probs = probs / probs.sum(-1, keepdim=True)

    return torch.multinomial(probs, 1)

With your Chapter 11 Shakespeare model, generate 500 characters at T ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.3, 2.0}. For each, measure: distinct-token ratio (unique/total), longest repeated substring, and your own 1–5 readability score.

What you'll see

T=0.1 produces loops and a low distinct ratio. T=2.0 produces high diversity and non-words. The readability peak is usually 0.7–0.9, and — importantly — the diversity and quality metrics peak at different temperatures. There is no single best T; there's a trade-off curve, and where you sit on it should be a product decision.

13.3Reproduce neural text degenerationthinking

Using GPT-2, generate 200 tokens from the same prompt with (a) greedy and (b) top-p 0.95. Then compute, for both outputs and for a real human paragraph, the average −log p the model assigns to each token.

The result, and why it matters

Greedy text has much lower average surprisal than human text. Nucleus text sits close to the human value. This is Holtzman et al.'s central figure: human language is not the most probable language. Maximising likelihood at generation time moves you away from the distribution you trained to match — a genuinely counterintuitive result, and the reason nobody uses greedy decoding for prose.

13.4Price best-of-nthinking

A coding task: 2,000-token prompt, 400-token answers, unit tests as a perfect verifier, each sample passes with p = 0.3. Assume a decoded token costs 4× a prefill token (Chapter 18 derives a ratio of this size).

  1. How many samples for a 95% chance that at least one passes?
  2. Cost of that best-of-n, in "prefill-token equivalents", with and without prefix sharing.
  3. When would you rather spend the same budget on a bigger model?
Answers
1. 1 − 0.7^n ≥ 0.95  ->  n ≥ ln 0.05 / ln 0.7 = 8.4  ->  n = 9   (0.960)
2. one sample:                2000 + 4 × 400  =  3,600
   9 samples, no sharing:     9 × 3,600       = 32,400

13.6Predict, then measure: batch-dependent outputexploration

Prediction first: with your Chapter 11 model in fp32 on CPU, greedy-decode one prompt (a) alone and (b) as row 0 of a batch of 16 different prompts (left-padded, Chapter 5). Will the 200 generated tokens be identical? Write your guess down. Then run it, and repeat in bf16 and, if you have one, on a GPU.

What to expect

Often identical on CPU in fp32 for a small model, because its logit gaps are large next to fp32 noise. Differences become likely in bf16 (about 3 significant digits, Chapter 0), on GPUs whose kernels pick tile shapes by batch size, and in longer generations, where one near-tie is enough. Record the first position where (a) and (b) differ and the top-2 logit gap there; it should be tiny. That gap, not "randomness", is the mechanism.

13.7Build a grammar mask for real JSONhard

Extend the toy Pattern to a pushdown automaton for the object {"name": <string>, "age": <integer>}, run it over a real vocabulary (GPT-2's 50,257 tokens), and time one mask computation. How many tokens are legal right after {"name": "? How many right after the closing quote of the name?

What you should find

Inside a string almost the whole vocabulary is legal (any token without an unescaped quote or control character), so the mask is nearly all ones; after the closing quote only a handful survive (a comma, or comma-space merges). In Python the mask will take milliseconds per step, longer than a small model's forward pass on a GPU: exactly the overhead XGrammar's precomputed context-independent masks remove.
