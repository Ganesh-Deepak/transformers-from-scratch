Do you have the prerequisites?

This course assumes surprisingly little, but it does assume some things. Check yourself honestly against this list — the fastest way to have a bad time is to start with a gap you haven’t noticed.

You needConcretely, you can…If not
Python write a function, a loop, a class; use a list comprehension; read a traceback Do a Python basics course first. This one won’t teach it.
Arrays index a nested list; know what a[2][0] means Chapter 1 covers the rest from zero.
School algebra rearrange y = mx + b; read a summation Σ; know what a logarithm is Chapter 1 has a refresher box for each.
Nothing else — Calculus, linear algebra and neural-network experience are not required. Where a chapter needs one, it derives it.
You do not need to know calculus

People assume deep learning requires multivariable calculus. For understanding and building a transformer, it does not — the derivatives are computed for you by autograd. Where gradients matter to the argument (Chapter 4’s residual stream, Chapter 8’s pre-LN, Chapter 20’s DPO) the course shows the one line of calculus that matters and explains what it means. You need to read it, not derive it.

How to work through this

Chapters are ordered by dependency, not by topic. Each one uses what the last one built, and the margin column tells you where an idea came from and where it goes next.

The margin Cross-references live out here, so you can see how a chapter connects without losing your place in the main column.
  1. Work in order. Parts 0 and I look elementary. Do not skip them. Almost everyone who finds transformers confusing is actually confused about tensor shapes.
  2. Drag the sliders. The interactive panels are the point, not decoration. Reading one is not the same as using it.
  3. Answer the checkpoints before revealing. Being wrong and noticing is the mechanism that makes this stick. Guessing then reading the explanation works; reading the explanation first does not.
  4. Do the exercises in the notebook, not in your head. The gap between “I understand attention” and “my attention passes the causality test” is where the learning is.

If a chapter isn’t landing

Do not push forward — the dependency chain means the next chapter will be worse. Instead: (1) open the companion notebook and run the code, changing numbers until the shapes make sense; (2) re-read the chapter that this one lists as its prerequisite; (3) try the exercise you skipped. Confusion in Chapter 6 is almost always an unfinished Chapter 1.

The five shapes in every transformer

tokens        (B, T)                        # int64 token ids
embeddings    (B, T, d_model)               # the residual stream
q, k, v       (B, n_head, T, d_head)        # after the reshape dance
attn weights  (B, n_head, T, T)             # rows sum to 1
logits        (B, T, V)                     # one distribution per position
On accuracy

Architectural numbers (DeepSeek-V3 MLA ranks, GQA head counts, FlashAttention versions, Chinchilla coefficients) come from primary papers and public model configs, checked August 2026. Every quantitative claim the course makes about its own code is backed by a test in tests/test_tfs.py. Where something is contested or open, the text says so rather than stating it flatly. Chapter 18 is deliberately dated and will age; Chapters 1–17 cover mechanisms that have been stable for years.
