20 · DPO From First Principles

Direct Preference Optimization replaces four models, a generation loop, and PPO's hyperparameter thicket with a single loss function you can write in six lines. It is not a heuristic or an approximation — it is derived from exactly the same objective PPO optimises. This chapter does the derivation completely.

Builds onCh 19 — Bradley-Terry and the KL-constrained objective
Leads toCh 21 — the variants, and what replaced it for reasoning
Why this chapterYour language model is secretly a reward model. Eight lines of algebra prove it.
The claim

Rafailov et al. (2023) proved that your language model is secretly a reward model. Given the RLHF objective, the optimal policy and the reward function are related by a closed-form equation. Invert it, substitute into the Bradley-Terry likelihood, and the reward model disappears — leaving a supervised classification loss on preference pairs.

20.1 · Setup: the objective we are actually solving

From Chapter 19, RLHF maximises:

maxπ   Ex∼D, y∼π(·|x)[ r(x,y) ] − β · KL(π(·|x) ‖ πref(·|x))

Notation for the rest of the chapter:

πθ the policy being trained πref frozen SFT model r(x,y) reward β KL strength (0.1 typical) yw, yl chosen / rejected

20.2 · Step 1 — the optimal policy has a closed form

Expand the KL and work with a single prompt x:

maxπ Ey∼π[ r(x,y) − β log π(y|x)πref(y|x) ]

Divide by β and flip the sign to get a minimisation:

minπ Ey∼π[ log π(y|x)πref(y|x) − 1β r(x,y) ]  =  minπ Ey∼π[ log π(y|x)πref(y|x) · er(x,y)/β ]

The denominator is a non-negative function of y, but it isn't a probability distribution — it doesn't sum to 1. Define the normaliser (the partition function):

Z(x) = Σy πref(y|x) · er(x,y)/β a sum over every possible completion y — astronomically intractable. Remember this.

Now divide and multiply by Z(x):

minπ Ey∼π[ log π(y|x)1Z(x)πref(y|x)er(x,y)/β − log Z(x) ]

The first term is now exactly KL(π ‖ π*) where π* is the distribution in the denominator; the second doesn't depend on π at all. A KL divergence is minimised (at zero) precisely when the two distributions are equal. Therefore:

π*(y|x) = 1Z(x) πref(y|x) · exp(1β r(x,y)) the exact optimum of the KL-constrained RLHF objective
Read what this says

The optimal policy is the reference policy re-weighted exponentially by reward. High-reward completions get up-weighted, low-reward ones down-weighted, and β controls how aggressively. At β→∞ you get πref back; at β→0 you get all mass on the argmax reward. This result is standard in the KL-regularised RL literature — it is not new to DPO. What DPO does next is.

20.3 · Step 2 — invert it

Solve the same equation for r. Take logs of both sides and rearrange:

r(x,y) = β log π*(y|x)πref(y|x) + β log Z(x)

This is the sentence in the chapter's opening. Any policy implicitly defines a reward function. The log-ratio between a policy and its reference is a reward, up to a prompt-dependent constant.

So we could parameterise the reward by the policy itself:

rθ(x,y) = β log πθ(y|x)πref(y|x) + β log Z(x)

…except Z(x) requires summing over every possible completion. Intractable. This looks like a dead end.

20.4 · Step 3 — the partition function cancels

Here is the trick. Recall from Chapter 19 that Bradley-Terry depends only on the difference of two rewards:

P(yw ≻ yl | x) = σ( r(x,yw) − r(x,yl) )

Substitute our expression for r into that difference:

r(x,yw) − r(x,yl) = [β log πθ(yw|x)πref(yw|x) + β log Z(x)] − [β log πθ(yl|x)πref(yl|x) + β log Z(x)]
= β log πθ(yw|x)πref(yw|x) − β log πθ(yl|x)πref(yl|x)
This is the whole paper

Z(x) depends only on the prompt, not on the completion. Both responses in a preference pair share the same prompt. So the intractable term appears identically in both and cancels exactly. No approximation, no bound, no sampling — it is simply gone.

20.5 · The DPO loss

Plug that difference into the Bradley-Terry negative log-likelihood:

ℒDPO = − E(x,yw,yl)∼D [ log σ β log πθ(yw|x)πref(yw|x) − β log πθ(yl|x)πref(yl|x) ) ]

No reward model. No value model. No generation during training. No RL. It is a binary classification loss over pairs, trained exactly like SFT.

In code

def dpo_loss(policy_chosen_logps, policy_rejected_logps,
             ref_chosen_logps,    ref_rejected_logps, beta=0.1):
    """All arguments are (B,) — SUMMED log-probs of the full response."""
    pi_logratios  = policy_chosen_logps - policy_rejected_logps
    ref_logratios = ref_chosen_logps    - ref_rejected_logps

    logits = pi_logratios - ref_logratios          # the "implicit reward margin"
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(beta * logits).mean()

    # Useful diagnostics — log these, they tell you what's happening
    chosen_rewards   = beta * (policy_chosen_logps   - ref_chosen_logps).detach()
    rejected_rewards = beta * (policy_rejected_logps - ref_rejected_logps).detach()
    accuracy = (chosen_rewards > rejected_rewards).float().mean()
    return loss, chosen_rewards, rejected_rewards, accuracy


def sequence_logp(model, ids, labels):
    """Sum of log P(token) over the RESPONSE tokens only."""
    logits = model(ids).logits[:, :-1]                       # (B, T-1, V)
    labels = labels[:, 1:]                                   # shift
    mask = labels != -100
    labels = labels.masked_fill(~mask, 0)
    per_token = torch.gather(logits.log_softmax(-1), 2,
                             labels.unsqueeze(2)).squeeze(2) # (B, T-1)
    return (per_token * mask).sum(-1)                        # (B,)
Three implementation details that decide whether it works
  1. Sum log-probs, don't average. The derivation is about log π(y|x) = Σt log π(yt|x, y<t) — a sum. Averaging by length changes the objective (that variant is SimPO, Chapter 21, and it is a deliberate different choice, not a bug fix).
  2. Mask the prompt. Only response tokens count, exactly as in SFT.
  3. πref must be frozen and in eval mode. Compute its log-probs under torch.no_grad(). Better: precompute them once for the whole dataset and cache — it halves memory and time, since the reference never changes.

20.6 · What the gradient does

Differentiating the loss gives:

∇θℒ = −β E [ σ(r̂l − r̂w) · ∇ log π(yw|x) − ∇ log π(yl|x) ) ] r̂ = the implicit reward β·log(π/πref)

Three parts, each meaningful:

  • Increase the likelihood of the chosen response.
  • Decrease the likelihood of the rejected response.
  • Weight by how wrong the model currently is. If the implicit reward already ranks the pair correctly, σ(r̂l−r̂w) is near 0 and this example contributes almost nothing. Pairs the model gets wrong dominate the gradient.

That adaptive weighting is a genuinely nice property — it's automatic hard-example mining, with no curriculum needed.

Why does the intractable partition function Z(x) disappear?
It's approximated by sampling
It equals 1 for a normalised policy
It depends only on the prompt, and Bradley-Terry uses the difference of two rewards for the same prompt — so it cancels exactly
It's absorbed into β
Exact cancellation. Both responses in a pair condition on the same x, so both carry the identical β log Z(x) term and it subtracts away. This is the entire insight of the paper — no approximation is involved anywhere.
What does β control in DPO?
How far the policy may deviate from πref — it is the same KL coefficient as in PPO
The learning rate
The ratio of chosen to rejected examples
The temperature used during generation
The KL strength, inherited directly from the RLHF objective. Small β (0.01) permits large deviation and risks degeneration; large β (0.5) keeps the policy close to the SFT model and changes little. 0.1 is the standard starting point. Note it is not a learning rate, though its effects can look similar if you only watch the loss.
During DPO training you observe that both chosen and rejected log-probs are decreasing. Is something wrong?
Yes — chosen log-probs must increase
No — this is the normal, widely-reported behaviour. The loss only constrains the margin, and it is usually easier to push the rejected down than the chosen up
Yes — β is too large
No, but it means the data is mislabelled
Normal, and worth understanding. The loss depends only on the difference of log-ratios, so it is satisfied by lowering both as long as the rejected falls faster. This is the known likelihood displacement issue: pushed far enough, DPO can reduce probability mass on good responses and shift it to unrelated outputs. Monitor absolute chosen log-probs, not just the margin — and if they collapse, raise β or mix in an SFT loss term.

20.7 · DPO vs PPO — the honest comparison

PPO (RLHF)DPO
Models in memory42 (1 if you cache reference log-probs)
Generation during trainingyes — the slow partno
Implementation difficultyhighlow
Hyperparametersmany, interactingβ, lr
Data usageon-policy — fresh samples from the current modeloff-policy — a fixed dataset
Can exceed the data distributionyesno
Reported peak qualityhigher when done wellvery competitive, far easier
The real limitation of DPO: it is off-policy

PPO samples from the current policy, so it gets feedback about what the model actually does now. DPO learns from a fixed dataset generated by some other model. As training proceeds, the policy drifts away from the data distribution and the preference signal becomes less relevant to its current behaviour. Several careful studies (notably Xu et al. 2024) find PPO retains an edge on the hardest tasks for exactly this reason. The practical fix is iterative/online DPO: generate fresh completions with the current policy, label them with a reward model or judge, retrain, repeat — recovering much of the on-policy benefit while keeping DPO's simplicity.

20.8 · Exercises

20.1Derive it yourselfessential — do this on paper

Without looking back at §20.2–20.4, derive the DPO loss from the KL-constrained objective. You should be able to produce, in order:

  1. The closed-form optimal policy π*, including where Z(x) comes from.
  2. The inverted expression for r(x,y).
  3. The substitution into Bradley-Terry and the cancellation.
  4. The final loss.

If you can do this from memory, you understand DPO better than most people using it. It's about eight lines of algebra and it's worth the twenty minutes.

20.2Implement DPO and run itcode

SFT a small model, then DPO it on a preference dataset. Log per step: loss, mean chosen reward, mean rejected reward, reward margin, ranking accuracy, and absolute chosen/rejected log-probs.

  1. Does accuracy exceed 0.5 and climb? (It should reach 0.7–0.9.)
  2. Do both log-probs decrease? By how much?
  3. Sweep β ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 0.5}. Generate samples from each. Describe the difference.
What you should see

β=0.01: the margin grows fast, and generations often degrade — short, repetitive, or oddly terse. This is under-constrained drift. β=0.5: barely differs from the SFT model. β=0.1: the usual sweet spot. The lesson is that DPO's loss going down does not mean the model got better — you must look at generations. A beautiful loss curve with degenerate samples is the single most common DPO failure.

20.3Verify the implicit reward is a real reward modelthinking

After DPO, compute r̂(x,y) = β·(log π_θ(y|x) − log π_ref(y|x)) on a held-out preference set and measure its pairwise ranking accuracy — exactly as you would evaluate an explicit reward model.

Why this matters

You should get accuracy well above chance, often competitive with an explicitly trained Bradley-Terry model on the same data. That is the theory made empirical: DPO really does train a reward model — it just stores it in the policy's weights. This is also a genuinely useful technique — you can use a DPO'd model as a reranker for free, with no separate reward model to host.
