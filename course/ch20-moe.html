20 · Mixture of Experts

Chapter 8 established that two-thirds of a transformer's parameters live in the MLP, and that every token passes through all of them. A Mixture of Experts breaks that link: keep a very large number of parameters, but let each token use only a small, learned slice of them. It is how a 671-billion-parameter model trains and runs at roughly the per-token cost of a 37-billion one, and it is the architecture underneath most of the frontier: DeepSeek-V3 and R1, Qwen3, Llama 4, gpt-oss, Kimi K2. This chapter builds the layer, then follows it into the three places where it gets hard: balancing the load, moving tokens between GPUs, and serving it.

The whole chapter in five sentences

An MoE layer is N small MLPs plus a router that sends each token to k of them, so parameters scale with N and per-token compute with k. The router learns only through the gate weights that multiply the chosen experts' outputs, which is why the details of the gate matter. Left alone, routing collapses onto a few experts; auxiliary losses or a balancing bias keep the load even. At scale the experts live on different GPUs, and moving tokens to them and back (the all-to-all) is the cost that shapes every modern design. MoE saves FLOPs, not memory: every expert must be resident, and at real serving batch sizes nearly every expert is read on every step.

20.1 · Why sparsity: capacity without compute

Start from the two costs of a dense transformer with N parameters. Compute: a forward pass costs about 2N FLOPs per token and training about 6N (Chapter 21). Memory: all N parameters sit in GPU memory, plus optimizer state while training. In a dense model the two are welded together: to know more you must make every token pay for everything you know.

An MoE splits N into two numbers. Total parameters are everything stored: all experts in every layer. Active parameters are what one token actually multiplies against: attention, the embeddings, any shared experts, and the k routed experts it was sent to. Compute follows the active count; memory follows the total.

FLOPs per token ≈ 2 · Nactive      memory ∝ Ntotal DeepSeek-V3: Ntotal = 671 B, Nactive = 37 B  ·  5.5% of the model per token

Why would that be a good trade? Because what a model can store and what a token needs to use are different quantities. A model trained on code, French, chemistry and poetry has to hold all of it; any single token of Python needs very little of the French. A dense MLP charges every token for all of it anyway. An MoE lets the model grow its knowledge (total parameters) while each token pays only for the slice it uses (active parameters).

The empirical case was made in stages. Shazeer et al. (2017) showed sparsely-gated experts in LSTMs could reach 137 billion parameters. GShard (2020) brought them to transformers with top-2 routing. The Switch Transformer (2021) simplified to top-1 and reported reaching a dense T5 model's quality up to 7× faster at the same FLOPs per token. Since 2024 the open-weights frontier has been almost entirely MoE.

What "37B active" does and does not mean

All 671 B parameters are trained, and all of them must be in memory somewhere. "Active" is about arithmetic: each token's forward pass multiplies against about 37 B of them. It does not mean the model behaves like a 37 B dense model (it learns more like a much larger one), and it does not mean serving it is as cheap as serving a 37 B model. Section 20.7 shows why the second one surprises people.

20.2 · Anatomy of an MoE layer

An MoE layer replaces the MLP in a block (Chapter 8). The attention sublayer, the norms and the residual stream are untouched. The layer has three parts: E routed experts (each an ordinary SwiGLU MLP, usually much narrower than the dense one), a router (a single Linear(d_model, E), the whole decision mechanism), and optionally shared experts that every token passes through. Follow the shapes:

x                      (B, T, C)       the residual stream, as always
x.view(-1, C)          (N, C)          N = B*T: routing is PER TOKEN, batch and time are flattened away
router(x)              (N, E)          one logit per expert
top-k                  (N, k), (N, k)  gate weights w and expert ids idx
dispatch               (N*k, C)        each token copied once per chosen expert
expert e on its rows   (n_e, C)        n_e = how many slots chose e; sum over e of n_e = N*k
combine                (N, C)          out[token] = sum over its k slots of w * expert(x)
+ shared experts       (N, C)          every token, unconditionally
view back              (B, T, C)

Here is the readable version, straight from tfs/moe.py. The loop over experts is for clarity; section 20.3 replaces it with the layout production kernels use, and the test suite checks that the two give identical outputs.

class MoE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, d_ff, n_expert, top_k, n_shared=0):
        super().__init__()
        self.router  = nn.Linear(d_model, n_expert, bias=False)            # (C -> E)  tiny
        self.experts = nn.ModuleList(Expert(d_model, d_ff) for _ in range(n_expert))
        self.shared  = nn.ModuleList(Expert(d_model, d_ff) for _ in range(n_shared))
        self.top_k   = top_k

    def forward(self, x):                                   # x: (B, T, C)
        B, T, C = x.shape
        x = x.view(-1, C)                                   # (N, C)
        probs = self.router(x).float().softmax(-1)          # (N, E)   fp32 router (20.8)
        w, idx = probs.topk(self.top_k, dim=-1)             # (N, k), (N, k)
        w = (w / w.sum(-1, keepdim=True)).to(x.dtype)       # (N, k)   renormalised

        out = torch.zeros_like(x)                           # (N, C)
        for e, expert in enumerate(self.experts):
            hit  = idx == e                                 # (N, k)   bool
            rows = hit.any(-1)                              # (N,)     tokens that chose e
            if not rows.any(): continue
            gate = (w * hit).sum(-1)[rows].unsqueeze(-1)    # (n_e, 1)
            out[rows] += gate * expert(x[rows])             # (n_e, C)
        for s in self.shared:
            out += s(x)                                     # (N, C)   every token
        return out.view(B, T, C)
Routing is per token, per layer

The view(-1, C) is the whole story. Every token in the batch independently picks its own experts, and the choice is remade at every MoE layer. There is no "the maths expert" that a document is sent to. A single sentence touches dozens of different experts on its way through the network, and section 20.9's exercise shows the specialisation that does emerge is mostly syntactic, not topical.

Three things this makes concrete. (1) The decision is per token and remade at every layer. (2) The weights are soft: expert 1 contributes 1.6× more than expert 3. (3) Nothing here stops expert 1 winning for every token, which is the collapse section 20.4 is about.

Three routers, and the one fact that connects two of them

Real models use three different gates. The differences look cosmetic and are not.

RouterHow the k gates are computedUsed by
softmax, then top-ksoftmax over all E logits, keep the k largest probabilities (renormalised or not)GShard, Switch (top-1, not renormalised), DeepSeek-V2
top-k, then softmaxtake the k largest logits, softmax over those k onlyMixtral, gpt-oss
sigmoid + biasindependent sigmoid score per expert; a balancing bias decides which k, the unbiased scores (renormalised) decide how muchDeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2

The first two are the same function when the first is renormalised. For the chosen set K, renormalised softmax gives (eℓi/Z) / Σj∈K(eℓj/Z); the Z cancels and what is left is exactly the softmax over the k chosen logits. The test test_topk_softmax_equals_renormalised_softmax_topk checks it numerically. So when you read a model card that says "top-k then softmax", nothing new is happening.

Why would DeepSeek switch to sigmoid? With 256 experts a softmax makes every expert compete with every other: the probabilities are tiny, and pushing one up pushes all the others down. Independent sigmoid scores let each expert say "yes, this token is mine" without stealing from the rest, and they make it easy to add the balancing bias of section 20.4 to the selection only.

How the router learns at all

Choosing the top k is a sort: it is not differentiable. No gradient flows through "which experts were picked". The router's only path to a gradient is the gate weight w that multiplies each chosen expert's output. If routing a token to expert 1 with weight 0.62 lowers the loss, the gradient raises expert 1's logit for tokens like this one; the six experts that were not chosen receive exactly zero gradient for this token.

The top-1 trap: a router that never learns

With k = 1, renormalising the single chosen probability (or taking a softmax over one logit) makes every gate exactly 1. A constant has no gradient, so the router is frozen at its random initialisation forever. The test suite measures it: gradient 0.0 with the renormalised gate, 0.37 with the raw one. This is why the Switch Transformer multiplies the expert output by the raw probability p and does not renormalise. If you ever write a top-1 MoE and see routing that never changes, this is the first thing to check.

20.3 · Making it fast: dispatch, sort, grouped GEMM

The loop in 20.2 is correct and slow. Each x[rows] is a gather with a different, data-dependent size, each expert call is a separate small matmul, and on a GPU you pay kernel-launch overhead per expert per layer. With 256 experts that is 256 tiny matmuls where a dense MLP did one big one.

The fix is a layout change. Think of each token's k choices as k slots: there are N·k slots in total. Sort the slots by expert. Now every expert's tokens are one contiguous block, and the whole layer is one grouped GEMM: many matmuls of different heights, same width, launched together.

def forward(self, x):                                     # x: (B, T, C)
    x = x.reshape(-1, C)                                  # (N, C)
    w, idx = self.route(x)                                # (N, k), (N, k)
    flat_e = idx.reshape(-1)                              # (N*k,)  the expert of each slot
    order  = torch.argsort(flat_e, stable=True)           # (N*k,)  slots grouped by expert
    token  = order // self.k                              # (N*k,)  which token each slot came from
    counts = torch.bincount(flat_e, minlength=self.E)     # (E,)    block height per expert
    xs = x[token]                                         # (N*k, C)  DISPATCH: one gather
    ys = grouped_gemm(xs, counts, self.experts)           # (N*k, C)  one launch, E blocks
    ys = ys * w.reshape(-1)[order].unsqueeze(-1)          # (N*k, C)  apply the gates
    return torch.zeros_like(x).index_add_(0, token, ys)   # (N, C)    COMBINE: one scatter-add

tfs/moe.py implements exactly this (with a Python loop over the blocks standing in for the kernel), and test_moe_sorted_dispatch_matches_loop checks it against the readable version for all three routers. MegaBlocks, the grouped_gemm package and DeepSeek's DeepEP are production versions of these four lines.

Capacity, and the tokens that get dropped

Older systems (GShard, Switch) could not handle data-dependent block sizes, so they gave every expert a fixed buffer:

capacity = ⌈ capacity_factor · N · k / E ⌉ 4096 tokens, 64 experts, top-2, factor 1.25 → 160 slots per expert (the fair share is 128)

Slots that arrive after an expert's buffer is full are dropped: that token skips this expert and keeps only what the residual connection and its other experts give it. How many are dropped depends far more on balance than on the factor. Simulated in the notebook, with 64 experts, top-2 and 4096 tokens:

Capacity factorDropped, uniform routingDropped, mildly skewed router
1.003.4%26.2%
1.250.0%17.0%
1.500.0%10.0%
2.000.0%1.9%

The skew here is mild: a router logit offset of at most ±0.5 across experts. A quarter of the tokens are silently dropped at factor 1.0. That is the practical reason load balancing (next section) is not optional, and why modern systems went dropless: MegaBlocks expresses the layer as block-sparse matrix multiplication with no fixed buffers, and DeepSeek-V3 drops no tokens in training or inference. Raising the capacity factor instead costs memory and padded compute on every expert, every step.

20.4 · Load balancing: keeping all the experts alive

Left alone, routers collapse. An expert that is slightly better early is picked more, so it receives more gradient, so it gets better, so it is picked more. Within a few thousand steps a handful of experts do all the work and the rest are dead weight: all the memory cost of a huge model, the capacity of a small one. On top of that, in expert-parallel training (20.6) the busiest expert's GPU sets the step time for everyone.

Solution 1 · the auxiliary loss

ℒaux = α · E · Σi=1E fi · Pi fi = fraction of routed slots that went to expert i  ·  Pi = mean router probability of expert i  ·  α ≈ 0.01

Why a product of two quantities that both measure "how popular is expert i"? Because they play different roles. fi is a count: it measures the real load, but counts have no gradient. Pi is a mean of softmax outputs: it is differentiable. The product uses the count to decide how hard to push and the probability as the handle to push with: the gradient lowers the router probability of exactly the experts that are overloaded, in proportion to how overloaded they are.

The scaling by E makes the value interpretable. With perfectly uniform routing, fi = Pi = 1/E and the loss is exactly 1. If every token goes to one expert with probability 1, it is exactly E. A 4-expert router that always picks expert 0 with probability 0.7 scores 2.8. (All three are asserted in the notebook.)

The cost is that this term fights the language-modelling objective: sometimes the best expert for a token really is the busy one, and the aux loss pushes it elsewhere. Too large an α measurably hurts quality; exercise 20.1 makes you watch it happen.

Over what do you balance? It matters more than α.

Computing f and P over each micro-batch, or worse each sequence, forces every short window of text to use every expert evenly, which directly prevents specialisation: a Python file should lean on different experts than a poem. Qiu et al. (2025) showed that computing the balance over the global batch (synchronising the counts across data-parallel ranks) lets experts specialise by domain while staying balanced overall, and improved quality. Qwen3's MoE models use a global-batch balance loss.

Solution 2 · the router z-loss

A separate problem: router logits tend to grow. Large logits make the softmax spiky and brittle, and they overflow in low precision (Chapter 12). ST-MoE (Zoph et al., 2022) added a small penalty on their size:

ℒz = mean over tokens of ( log Σj eℓj )2 pushes the router's log-partition toward 0 · coefficient ~1e-3 · keep the router itself in fp32

It does not change which expert wins; it keeps the scores in a numerically safe range. Together with running the router in fp32 even when everything else is bf16 or fp8 (the Switch paper's "selective precision"), it removes most MoE training instabilities.

Solution 3 · auxiliary-loss-free balancing (DeepSeek-V3)

DeepSeek's insight (Wang et al., 2024) is to balance by steering selection rather than by adding a loss. Each expert gets a bias bi that is added to its score only when choosing the top k. The gate weight that multiplies the expert's output uses the unbiased score. The bias is not learned by gradient descent at all: after each step, a fixed rule nudges it.

s   = router(x).sigmoid()                        # (N, E)  affinity per expert, independent
idx = (s + bias).topk(k, dim=-1).indices         # (N, k)  the bias decides WHO is chosen
w   = s.gather(-1, idx)                          # (N, k)  but the gate uses the UNBIASED score
w   = w / w.sum(-1, keepdim=True)                # (N, k)  normalised over the chosen k

# after each optimizer step, outside autograd:
load = torch.bincount(idx.flatten(), minlength=E)            # (E,)
bias += gamma * torch.sign(load.float().mean() - load)       # under-used up, over-used down
Why this works better

The bias changes which experts a token visits, never how much the visited experts' outputs count, and it adds nothing to the loss. So there is no term fighting the language-modelling objective. In the notebook, a router heavily skewed toward four of sixteen experts starts with a load coefficient of variation of 1.36 (very unbalanced); after 50 bias updates it is 0.15, and it stays there, with the gate weights provably unchanged. DeepSeek-V3 used gamma = 0.001 for most of training, plus a very small sequence-wise balance loss (α = 0.0001) as a safety net against extreme imbalance within single sequences.

A different idea: let the experts choose

Expert-choice routing (Zhou et al., 2022) turns the problem around: instead of each token picking k experts, each expert picks its top-c tokens from the batch. Load is perfectly balanced by construction, and a token can be processed by a variable number of experts. The catch is fatal for language models: whether your token is chosen depends on every other token in the batch, including tokens that come later in the sequence. That breaks causality, and at decode time there is only one new token, so there is nothing to choose among. Expert choice is used in encoders and vision models; autoregressive LLMs route by token choice.

20.5 · Fine-grained and shared experts

Two refinements from DeepSeekMoE (Dai et al., 2024) changed what MoE layers look like.

Fine-grained experts: more, smaller experts

Take Mixtral's shape, 8 experts with top-2. Split every expert into 4 narrower ones (a quarter of the hidden width each) and route each token to 8 of the resulting 32. Total parameters are unchanged; active parameters are unchanged; FLOPs are unchanged. What changes is the number of distinct combinations a token can use:

LayoutCombinations C(E, k)
8 experts, top-2 (Mixtral)28
32 experts, top-8 (each 1/4 the size)10,518,300
256 experts, top-8 (DeepSeek-V3)4.1 × 1014

More combinations means the model can assemble a much more specific computation for each token out of smaller parts, instead of choosing between a few large monoliths. Krajewski et al. (2024) made this quantitative: granularity behaves like a scaling hyperparameter, and the best granularity grows with the compute budget. It is not free. The router grows as d_model × E, sorting and dispatch work grows with E, each expert's matmul gets smaller (and less efficient on a GPU), and in expert parallelism a token's k slots scatter across more devices. 256 experts with top-8 is roughly where the frontier has settled; Kimi K2 pushed to 384.

Shared experts: a dense core that everyone uses

Some knowledge is needed by almost every token: syntax, common words, basic arithmetic. If routed experts must each learn it, it is duplicated across all of them. DeepSeekMoE designates one or two shared experts that every token passes through unconditionally, so that common knowledge lives once and the routed experts can specialise.

out = shared_expert(x)                          # (N, C)  every token, always
for e, g in zip(top8_routed_ids, gates):        # 8 of 256 routed experts per token
    out = out + g * expert[e](x)                # (N, C)

Whether shared experts are worth it is not settled. DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2 and Llama 4 use one; Qwen3's MoE models removed them (Qwen2.5-MoE had them). Treat this as a live design choice, not a law.

20.6 · Expert parallelism and the all-to-all

A 256-expert layer does not fit, and should not be replicated, on one GPU. Expert parallelism (EP) shards the experts across devices: with 64 GPUs in the EP group, each holds 4 experts of every MoE layer. Attention and everything else are parallelised the usual ways (Chapter 19). The MoE layer then becomes three phases:

  1. Dispatch: every device routes its own tokens, then sends each slot to the device holding that expert. Every device sends to every other device: an all-to-all.
  2. Compute: each device runs its experts on whatever arrived, as one grouped GEMM.
  3. Combine: a second all-to-all sends results back to the tokens' home devices, where they are weighted and summed.

The bill is easy to compute and sobering. One DeepSeek-V3-shaped layer (d_model = 7168, top-8), with 4096 tokens per device, dispatching in fp8 and combining in bf16:

4096 · 8 · 7168 · (1 + 2) bytes = 0.70 GB per layer per device × 58 MoE layers = 40.9 GB per forward pass · 45 ms at NVLink's 900 GB/s · 817 ms at InfiniBand's 50 GB/s

Within one 8-GPU node that is tolerable. Across nodes, on InfiniBand, it would dominate the step. Every production design is about this number:

  • Node-limited routing. DeepSeek-V3 restricts each token to experts on at most 4 nodes, choosing nodes first by their best experts' scores. A token is then sent across InfiniBand at most once per node and fanned out to the GPUs inside the node over NVLink, so the slow link carries at most 4 copies instead of 8.
  • Overlap. The all-to-all of one micro-batch runs while another micro-batch computes. DeepSeek's DualPipe schedule and DeepEP kernels exist to hide communication behind compute.
  • Balance is speed. The all-to-all is a barrier: every device waits for the slowest. An expert that gets 2× its share makes its GPU, and therefore the whole layer, 2× slower. In EP, load balancing is a throughput requirement, not just a quality one.
  • Low-precision dispatch. Sending activations in fp8 halves the dispatch volume (Chapter 0's formats paying their way again).

20.7 · Serving MoE: the bill comes due

Memory first. Every expert must be resident somewhere. DeepSeek-V3 is 671 GB in fp8 or 1.34 TB in bf16: about 9 or 17 H100s just to hold the weights, before a single KV-cache byte. A dense 37B model fits on one GPU. "Cheap to run, expensive to host" is the MoE trade, and it is why MoE is a datacentre architecture.

Then bandwidth, and here is the part that surprises people. Chapter 14 showed that decoding is memory-bandwidth bound: each step must read the weights it uses from HBM. With batch size 1, a token reads only its k experts, a tiny fraction of the layer. But servers do not decode one token at a time. With a batch of b tokens, each picking k of E experts, the expected number of distinct experts touched is:

E · ( 1 − (1 − k/E)b ) each expert is missed by one token with probability 1 − k/E, so by all b tokens with (1 − k/E)b
Model (E, top-k)b = 1b = 8b = 32b = 64b = 256

Qwen3-235B (128, top-8)6%40%87%98%100%

(Percent of routed experts read per layer per step, assuming uniform routing; the formula is checked against simulation in the tests. Real routing is correlated, which helps a little.)

At serving batch sizes, an MoE reads nearly all of itself

Mixtral's sparsity is gone at a batch of 8. DeepSeek-V3 reads 87% of its routed experts at a batch of 64. So the bandwidth saving people expect from "37B active" mostly evaporates in batched decoding: per step you stream most of the 671B anyway. What MoE still saves at large batch is FLOPs (each token multiplies against 8 experts, not 256) and that is what matters once the batch is big enough to be compute-bound. This is why DeepSeek serves V3 with very wide expert parallelism: spread the experts over many GPUs so each GPU holds few experts and each expert sees a large batch of tokens, turning a bandwidth problem back into a compute problem.

Making it fit. The other levers are the ones from Chapters 0, 12 and 18: quantize the experts (gpt-oss ships its MoE weights in MXFP4, which is how a 117B model fits on one 80 GB GPU), offload cold experts to CPU memory for local inference (slow, because the expert you need is often not resident), and keep attention and the shared expert at higher precision than the routed experts.

20.8 · Training MoE without it falling over

  • Router in fp32, plus the z-loss. The router is tiny, so this costs nothing, and router logits are where low precision bites first.
  • Dense first layers. DeepSeek-V3 keeps its first 3 of 61 layers dense; Llama 4 alternates dense and MoE layers. Early layers do general-purpose work that gains little from routing, and dense layers there make early training more stable.
  • Initialise the router small so early routing is near-uniform and every expert receives gradient before any can win the rich-get-richer race.
  • Fine-tuning is touchier than dense. A new data distribution shifts routing, some experts get almost no gradient, and results are noisier. Common practice: a lower learning rate for the router, or freezing it, and keeping some load-balancing pressure on.
  • Upcycling. You can start an MoE from a dense checkpoint: copy the trained MLP into every expert, add a fresh router, and continue training (Komatsuzaki et al., 2022). The experts then diverge. It is a cheap way to get a strong MoE from an existing dense model, at the cost of experts that start identical.
  • MoE composes with everything else. DeepSeek-V3 uses MLA (Chapter 16) for attention and MoE for the MLP: one attacks the KV cache, the other per-token FLOPs. They are orthogonal.

Mixtral's experts are Mistral-7B's SwiGLU (4096 × 14336): eight of them over 32 layers are 45.1 B of its 46.7 B total, two of them are 11.3 B of its 12.9 B active; attention and embeddings are the rest. The trend since 2024 is unmistakable: more experts, finer grain, sparser activation. DeepSeek-V3 activates 5.5% of its parameters, gpt-oss-120b 4.4%, Kimi K2 about 3%.

Why does DeepSeek-V3 limit each token to experts on at most 4 nodes?
To reduce the number of parameters each node stores
To cap how many copies of each token cross the slow inter-node link in the all-to-all
To make the router cheaper to compute
Because top-8 routing cannot address more than 4 nodes
InfiniBand is ~18× slower than NVLink. With node-limited routing a token crosses the network at most once per target node and is fanned out inside the node over NVLink, capping the slow traffic at 4 copies per token instead of 8.
Why can't GPT-style models simply use expert-choice routing, which balances load perfectly?
Whether a token is chosen depends on other tokens in the batch, including future ones, which breaks causality and fails at single-token decode
It needs more experts than fit in memory
It is not differentiable
It requires sigmoid gating
It leaks the future. Experts choose their top tokens from the whole batch, so a token's computation depends on tokens after it. At decode time there is a single new token per sequence and nothing to choose among.
Why do fine-grained designs use 256 small experts with top-8 rather than 8 large experts with top-1?
Smaller experts train faster
It uses less total memory
Top-8 routing is cheaper to compute than top-1
Far more expert combinations for the same active parameters, enabling more specific computation per token
Combinatorics. C(256, 8) ≈ 4.1 × 1014 subsets versus 8. Total memory and active FLOPs are comparable; routing and dispatch are slightly more expensive, and the gain is expressiveness.

20.10 · The mental model to keep

  • Two parameter counts. Total decides memory and what the model can know. Active decides FLOPs per token. Ask for both whenever someone quotes one.
  • The router is a Linear and a sort. It learns only through the gate weights of the experts it chose. Constant gates mean a frozen router.
  • Balance is three problems. Quality (dead experts), throughput (the slowest expert sets the step time) and dropped tokens (fixed capacity). Aux loss and the DeepSeek bias address the first two; dropless kernels remove the third.
  • The all-to-all is the real cost of scale. Count its bytes: tokens × k × d_model × bytes, twice. Node-limited routing, overlap and fp8 dispatch all exist to shrink or hide it.
  • Sparsity saves FLOPs, not memory, and less bandwidth than you think. At batch b a layer reads E(1 − (1 − k/E)b) experts.

20.3Prove the top-1 trap, then fix itessential

Build a top-1 MoE with a renormalised gate and train it for a few hundred steps. Log the router weight's gradient norm and the routing decisions of a fixed set of 1000 tokens.

  1. Show that the gradient norm is zero and the routing never changes.
  2. Switch to the Switch Transformer gate (multiply by the raw probability p, no renormalisation) and show both start to move.
  3. Explain in one sentence why top-2 with renormalisation does not have this problem.
Answer to 3

With two chosen experts the gates are p1/(p1+p2) and p2/(p1+p2), which vary with the logits, so the router gets a gradient for the relative preference between the two it chose. It still gets nothing for the experts it did not choose.

20.4Size a serving deploymentthinking

You serve a 256-expert, top-8 MoE with 58 MoE layers, routed experts of 44 M parameters each, stored in fp8, on GPUs with 3.35 TB/s of HBM bandwidth. Ignore attention and the KV cache.

  1. Bytes of routed-expert weights read per decode step at batch 1, 16, 64 and 256 (use expected_distinct_experts).
  2. The bandwidth-bound time per step for each, on a single GPU that held everything (it could not; this is a lower bound).
  3. Tokens per second per step at each batch size. Where does adding batch stop being nearly free?
How to read your answer

Bytes per step grow quickly with batch until almost every expert is read, then stop growing. Throughput (tokens per step ÷ time per step) therefore rises almost linearly with batch at first and keeps rising after the weights are saturated, because the weights are read once for the whole batch. That is the whole economics of batched MoE serving, and the reason serving systems push batch size and expert parallelism as high as latency allows.

20.6Show that expert choice leaks the futureexploration

Implement expert-choice routing: each of E experts takes its top c = N·k/E tokens by router score. Build a batch, record which experts process token 5 of sequence 0, then change only token 9 of that sequence and route again.

What you'll see

Token 5's set of experts can change even though nothing at or before position 5 changed: a later token outbid it for an expert's capacity. In a causal model that is information flowing backwards in time, and it is why decoder LLMs route by token choice.

20.7Do experts actually specialise?exploration

Load a small open MoE (Mixtral-8x7B, or any open MoE you can run). Feed it text from several domains (Python, French, mathematics, poetry) and record the router's expert choices at each layer. Build a heatmap of domain × expert for an early, a middle and a late layer.

What the literature finds

Specialisation is real but weaker and more syntactic than expected. The Mixtral paper found routing correlates with token identity and local syntax (indentation, punctuation, numerals) more than with topic, and consecutive tokens often go to the same expert. Balancing over the global batch rather than per sequence (20.4) makes domain specialisation stronger. Think of experts as specialised computational routines, not topic specialists.
