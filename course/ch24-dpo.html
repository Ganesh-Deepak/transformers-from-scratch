24 · DPO From First Principles

Direct Preference Optimization replaces four models, a generation loop and PPO's tangle of hyperparameters with a single loss function you can write in six lines. It is not a heuristic or an approximation. It is derived from exactly the same objective that PPO optimises. This chapter works through the whole derivation.

Why this chapterYour language model is secretly a reward model. Eight lines of algebra prove it. The online-DPO numbers in 24.8 come from tfs/dpo.py and are asserted in tests/test_posttraining.py.
The claim

Rafailov et al. (2023) proved that your language model is secretly a reward model. Given the RLHF objective, the optimal policy and the reward function are linked by a closed-form equation. Invert it, substitute it into the Bradley-Terry likelihood, and the reward model disappears. What is left is a supervised classification loss on preference pairs.

24.1 · Setup: the objective we are actually solving

From Chapter 23, RLHF maximises:

maxπ   Ex∼D, y∼π(·|x)[ r(x,y) ] − β · KL(π(·|x) ‖ πref(·|x))

Notation for the rest of the chapter:

πθ the policy being trained πref frozen SFT model r(x,y) reward β KL strength (0.1 typical) yw, yl chosen / rejected

24.2 · Step 1 — the optimal policy has a closed form

Expand the KL and work with a single prompt x:

maxπ Ey∼π[ r(x,y) − β log π(y|x)πref(y|x) ]

Divide by β and flip the sign to get a minimisation:

minπ Ey∼π[ log π(y|x)πref(y|x) − 1β r(x,y) ]  =  minπ Ey∼π[ log π(y|x)πref(y|x) · er(x,y)/β ]

The denominator is a non-negative function of y, but it is not a probability distribution, because it does not sum to 1. Define the normaliser (the partition function):

Z(x) = Σy πref(y|x) · er(x,y)/β a sum over every possible completion y — astronomically intractable. Remember this.

Now divide and multiply by Z(x):

minπ Ey∼π[ log π(y|x)1Z(x)πref(y|x)er(x,y)/β − log Z(x) ]

The first term is now exactly KL(π ‖ π*), where π* is the distribution in the denominator. The second term does not depend on π at all. A KL divergence reaches its minimum (zero) exactly when the two distributions are equal. So:

π*(y|x) = 1Z(x) πref(y|x) · exp(1β r(x,y)) the exact optimum of the KL-constrained RLHF objective
Read what this says

The optimal policy is the reference policy re-weighted exponentially by reward. High-reward completions get more weight, low-reward ones less, and β controls how strongly. At β→∞ you get πref back, and at β→0 you get all the weight on the highest-reward completion. This result is standard in the KL-regularised RL literature and is not new to DPO. What DPO does next is new.

24.3 · Step 2 — invert it

Solve the same equation for r. Take logs of both sides and rearrange:

r(x,y) = β log π*(y|x)πref(y|x) + β log Z(x)

This is the claim from the start of the chapter. Any policy quietly defines a reward function. The log-ratio between a policy and its reference is a reward, up to a constant that depends on the prompt.

So we could parameterise the reward by the policy itself:

rθ(x,y) = β log πθ(y|x)πref(y|x) + β log Z(x)

…except that Z(x) requires summing over every possible completion, which is impossible in practice. It looks like a dead end.

24.4 · Step 3 — the partition function cancels

Here is the trick. Recall from Chapter 23 that Bradley-Terry depends only on the difference of two rewards:

P(yw ≻ yl | x) = σ( r(x,yw) − r(x,yl) )

Substitute our expression for r into that difference:

r(x,yw) − r(x,yl) = [β log πθ(yw|x)πref(yw|x) + β log Z(x)] − [β log πθ(yl|x)πref(yl|x) + β log Z(x)]
= β log πθ(yw|x)πref(yw|x) − β log πθ(yl|x)πref(yl|x)
This is the whole paper

Z(x) depends only on the prompt, not on the completion. Both responses in a preference pair share the same prompt. So the intractable term appears identically in both and cancels exactly. There is no approximation, no bound and no sampling. It is simply gone.

24.5 · The DPO loss

Plug that difference into the Bradley-Terry negative log-likelihood:

ℒDPO = − E(x,yw,yl)∼D [ log σ( β log πθ(yw|x)πref(yw|x) − β log πθ(yl|x)πref(yl|x) ) ]

No reward model. No value model. No generation during training. No RL. It is a binary classification loss over pairs, trained just like SFT.

In code

def dpo_loss(policy_chosen_logps, policy_rejected_logps,
             ref_chosen_logps,    ref_rejected_logps, beta=0.1):
    """All arguments are (B,) — SUMMED log-probs of the full response."""
    pi_logratios  = policy_chosen_logps - policy_rejected_logps
    ref_logratios = ref_chosen_logps    - ref_rejected_logps

    logits = pi_logratios - ref_logratios          # the "implicit reward margin"
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(beta * logits).mean()

    # Useful diagnostics — log these, they tell you what's happening
    chosen_rewards   = beta * (policy_chosen_logps   - ref_chosen_logps).detach()
    rejected_rewards = beta * (policy_rejected_logps - ref_rejected_logps).detach()
    accuracy = (chosen_rewards > rejected_rewards).float().mean()
    return loss, chosen_rewards, rejected_rewards, accuracy


def sequence_logp(model, ids, labels):
    """Sum of log P(token) over the RESPONSE tokens only."""
    logits = model(ids).logits[:, :-1]                       # (B, T-1, V)
    labels = labels[:, 1:]                                   # shift
    mask = labels != -100
    labels = labels.masked_fill(~mask, 0)
    per_token = torch.gather(logits.log_softmax(-1), 2,
                             labels.unsqueeze(2)).squeeze(2) # (B, T-1)
    return (per_token * mask).sum(-1)                        # (B,)
Three implementation details that decide whether it works
  1. Sum the log-probs; do not average them. The derivation is about log π(y|x) = Σt log π(yt|x, y<t), which is a sum. Averaging by length changes the objective. That variant is SimPO (Chapter 25), and it is a deliberate, different choice, not a bug fix.
  2. Mask the prompt. Only response tokens count, exactly as in SFT.
  3. πref must be frozen and in eval mode. Compute its log-probs under torch.no_grad(). Better still, compute them once for the whole dataset and cache them. The reference never changes, so this halves memory and time.

24.6 · What the gradient does

Differentiating the loss gives:

∇θℒ = −β E [ σ(r̂l − r̂w) · ( ∇ log π(yw|x) − ∇ log π(yl|x) ) ] r̂ = the implicit reward β·log(π/πref)

There are three parts, and each one means something:

  • Increase the likelihood of the chosen response.
  • Decrease the likelihood of the rejected response.
  • Weight by how wrong the model currently is. If the implicit reward already ranks the pair correctly, σ(r̂l−r̂w) is near 0 and this example adds almost nothing. Pairs the model gets wrong dominate the gradient.

That weighting is a really nice property. The loss focuses on hard examples by itself, with no curriculum needed.

Why does the intractable partition function Z(x) disappear?
It is approximated by sampling
It equals 1 for a normalised policy
It depends only on the prompt, and Bradley-Terry uses the difference of two rewards for the same prompt, so it cancels exactly
It is absorbed into β
Exact cancellation. Both responses in a pair condition on the same x, so both carry the identical β log Z(x) term, and it subtracts away. This is the whole insight of the paper, and no approximation is involved anywhere.
What does β control in DPO?
How far the policy may move away from πref; it is the same KL coefficient as in PPO
The learning rate
The ratio of chosen to rejected examples
The temperature used during generation
The KL strength, taken directly from the RLHF objective. A small β (0.01) allows large changes and risks the model falling apart. A large β (0.5) keeps the policy close to the SFT model and changes little. 0.1 is the standard starting point. Note that it is not a learning rate, though its effects can look similar if you only watch the loss.
During DPO training you observe that both chosen and rejected log-probs are decreasing. Is something wrong?
Yes: chosen log-probs must increase
No: this is the normal, widely reported behaviour. The loss only constrains the margin, and it is usually easier to push the rejected down than the chosen up
Yes: β is too large
No, but it means the data is mislabelled
Normal, and worth understanding. The loss depends only on the difference of log-ratios, so lowering both satisfies it, as long as the rejected falls faster. This is the known likelihood displacement issue. Pushed far enough, DPO can take probability away from good responses and move it to unrelated outputs. Watch the absolute chosen log-probs as well as the margin, and if they collapse, raise β or mix in an SFT loss term.

The real limitation of DPO: it is off-policy

PPO samples from the current policy, so it gets feedback about what the model actually does now. DPO learns from a fixed dataset generated by some other model. As training goes on, the policy drifts away from the data distribution, and the preference signal matters less to its current behaviour. Several careful studies (in particular Xu et al. 2024) find that PPO keeps an edge on the hardest tasks for exactly this reason. The practical fix is iterative/online DPO. Generate fresh completions with the current policy, label them with a reward model or judge, retrain, and repeat. That recovers much of the on-policy benefit while keeping DPO simple.

24.8 · Online and iterative DPO, in code

The box above names the fix, and this section builds it and measures it. Online DPO keeps DPO's loss and changes only where the pairs come from. Each round, sample from the current policy, label the samples with a reward model, a judge or a verifier, add the new pairs, train a little, and repeat.

def online_dpo(policy, ref, prompts, label, rounds, k=8, beta=0.1):
    pairs = []
    for _ in range(rounds):
        for x in prompts:
            ys = [policy.generate(x, temperature=1.0) for _ in range(k)]   # ON-policy samples
            best, worst = label(x, ys)                                    # RM, judge or verifier
            if best is not None:
                pairs.append((x, best, worst))
        ref_logps = precompute_reference_logps(ref, pairs)                # ref is still frozen
        train_dpo(policy, pairs, ref_logps, beta, steps=...)              # the loss of 24.5, unchanged

Two design choices hide in that loop. The first is which pairs. Best against worst of k samples gives the widest margin, while random pairs give more of them. The second is whether the reference moves. In iterative DPO as people run it (Xu et al. 2023; the Llama 3 and Tülu 3 recipes), each round's reference is often reset to the previous round's policy, so the KL limit is relative to where you are now. The toy below keeps πref fixed, so the comparison with offline DPO is clean.

A model small enough to see everything

Make the "language model" a single prompt with 8 possible responses and one logit per response. Response y has reward y/7. Like most SFT models, the reference policy puts most of its weight on mediocre responses: probabilities [0.30, 0.30, 0.20, 0.14, 0.02, 0.02, 0.01, 0.01], with expected reward 0.204. For this table-based policy the DPO gradient has a closed form, and it says something striking:

∂/∂θ ( log π(w) − log π(l) ) = ew − el   ⟹   ∇ℒ = −(β/N) Σi σ(−β mi) ( ewi − eli ) the softmax normaliser cancels in the difference, exactly as Z(x) did in 24.4 · mi = the implicit reward margin of pair i

Each pair moves only its own two logits, by equal and opposite amounts. A response that appears in no pair gets exactly zero gradient. (Its probability still changes, because the other logits move, but DPO never learns anything about it.) The test checks this closed form against autograd on dpo_loss. So offline DPO can only rearrange the responses its dataset happened to contain, and it spends its pairs on comparisons the reference made, not the ones the policy is making now.

Worked example — offline vs online DPO at the same budget

Both runs see 20 rounds × 8 samples = 160 samples. Each group of 8 is labelled as best against worst (20 pairs), and each run takes 200 gradient steps with β = 0.1 and learning rate 1.0. Only the sampling policy differs.

1
Offline: all 160 samples come from πref, then DPO trains on the 20 pairs. Typical pairs are (2, 0), (3, 0), (5, 0). Nearly every "rejected" answer is the reference's most common bad answer, so most of the signal says "not response 0". The policy learns that quickly and then has nothing more to learn from.
2
Online: each round samples 8 from the current policy. As the policy improves, its pairs move with it. (3, 2), (5, 2), (7, 1) appear: comparisons between the answers it now actually gives.

Same loss, same samples, same steps: only the source of the pairs changed. In a real model, generalisation softens the "zero gradient for unseen responses" result (raising one good answer raises similar ones), but the direction is the same. It is what Xu et al. (2024) and Tajwar et al. (2024) measured at scale: the gains of preference optimisation come from on-policy samples.

24.9 · DPO on reasoning traces

For problems with an answer that can be checked, you can build preference pairs with no human at all. Sample several chains of thought from the model, mark those with a correct final answer as chosen and the others as rejected, and run DPO. Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization (Pang et al. 2024) does this round after round, and reported Llama-2-70B-Chat rising from 55.6% to 81.6% on GSM8K. Three details matter, and each one follows from the derivation:

  • Add an NLL term on the chosen trace. The warning of section 24.6 hits hard on long traces. DPO can satisfy the margin by lowering both log-probabilities, and with hundreds of tokens per trace the chosen trace's likelihood can collapse. Pang et al. found that DPO alone did much worse than DPO plus a negative log-likelihood loss on the chosen trace (the same fix as ORPO, Ch 25).
  • Summed log-probs grow with length. A 2,000-token trace has a log-probability in the thousands of nats, so the margin β·(…) is large and noisy, and the loss carries a length signal. If correct traces tend to be shorter or longer than wrong ones, DPO learns length. Match lengths when you build pairs, or normalise.
  • It is still off-policy within a round. The pairs go stale as the model changes. That is why the method iterates, and why most labs now train reasoning with the on-policy RL of Chapter 25. DPO on traces is still the cheap option: no generation inside the training step, and no value model.

24.10 · Exercises

24.1Derive it yourselfessential — do this on paper

Without looking back at §24.2 to 24.4, derive the DPO loss from the KL-constrained objective. You should be able to produce, in order:

  1. The closed-form optimal policy π*, including where Z(x) comes from.
  2. The inverted expression for r(x,y).
  3. The substitution into Bradley-Terry and the cancellation.
  4. The final loss.

If you can do this from memory, you understand DPO better than most people who use it. It is about eight lines of algebra, and well worth the twenty minutes.

24.2Implement DPO and run itcode

SFT a small model, then DPO it on a preference dataset. Log per step: loss, mean chosen reward, mean rejected reward, reward margin, ranking accuracy, and absolute chosen/rejected log-probs.

  1. Does accuracy go above 0.5 and keep climbing? (It should reach 0.7 to 0.9.)
  2. Do both log-probs decrease? By how much?
  3. Sweep β ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 0.5}. Generate samples from each. Describe the difference.
What you should see

β=0.01: the margin grows fast, and generations often get worse: short, repetitive or oddly terse. This is drift with too little constraint. β=0.5: barely differs from the SFT model. β=0.1: usually the best setting. The lesson is that a falling DPO loss does not prove the model got better. You must look at generations. A beautiful loss curve with broken samples is the most common DPO failure.

24.3Verify the implicit reward is a real reward modelthinking

After DPO, compute r̂(x,y) = β·(log π_θ(y|x) − log π_ref(y|x)) on a held-out preference set and measure its pairwise ranking accuracy, exactly as you would evaluate an explicit reward model.

Why this matters

You should get accuracy well above chance, often as good as a Bradley-Terry model trained directly on the same data. That is the theory confirmed by experiment: DPO really does train a reward model. It just stores it in the policy's weights. This is also a useful technique in its own right. You can use a DPO-trained model as a reranker for free, with no separate reward model to host.

24.4Online DPO on a real (small) modelcode

Start from the SFT checkpoint of exercise 24.2 and a reward function you can compute (a trained reward model, or a rule such as "answers under 40 words that contain the key term"). Give offline and online DPO the same budget: R rounds × k samples per prompt, and the same number of gradient steps.

  1. Offline: sample every completion from the SFT model up front, build best-against-worst pairs, and train.
  2. Online: sample from the current policy each round, add that round's pairs, and train.
  3. Plot the mean reward of fresh samples and the mean KL to the SFT model after each round, for both. Reproduce the toy first with tfs.dpo.offline_dpo and online_dpo, so you know what the curves should look like.
What to look for

Online should reach a higher reward at a given KL, and keep improving after offline levels off. Compare reward at matched KL. The final reward alone can mislead. A run that drifts further from the reference can buy reward through exactly the reward hacking of Chapter 23, and the KL axis is how you spot it.
