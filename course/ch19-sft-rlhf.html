19 · SFT, RLHF, and PPO

A pretrained model is a text predictor, not an assistant. Ask it a question and it may well produce five more questions — because that's what documents containing questions look like. This part of the course is about closing the gap between "models the internet" and "does what you asked."

19.2 · Supervised fine-tuning

Mechanically, SFT is identical to pretraining — same loss, same optimizer. Two differences:

# 1. The data is formatted with a chat template (Chapter 3)
text = tokenizer.apply_chat_template([
    {"role": "user",      "content": "What is attention?"},
    {"role": "assistant", "content": "Attention lets each token..."}
], tokenize=False)

# 2. Loss is computed on the ASSISTANT tokens only.
labels = input_ids.clone()
labels[:len(prompt_ids)] = -100     # -100 = ignore_index in F.cross_entropy
Masking the prompt matters more than people expect

If you train on prompt tokens too, the model spends capacity learning to generate user questions — a task you never want it to perform. It also dilutes the gradient signal on what you actually care about. Always mask, and always print a decoded example with the masked positions marked before launching a run.

Why SFT isn't enough

  • It can only imitate, never exceed. SFT's ceiling is the quality of your demonstrations. If your annotators write mediocre answers, you get a mediocre model.
  • "Good" is easier to judge than to produce. Writing an excellent response is expensive; comparing two responses is cheap. Preference data is far more scalable than demonstration data.
  • There is no signal about what to avoid. SFT only ever says "do this". It never says "not that", so there's no gradient pushing away from unhelpful or unsafe outputs.
The core argument for preference learning

For most of what we want from an assistant — helpfulness, tone, honesty about uncertainty — we cannot write down a loss function, and we cannot reliably produce optimal demonstrations. But we can reliably say which of two responses is better. Preference optimisation is the machinery for turning that comparison signal into a gradient.

19.3 · The reward model

Take a pretrained LM, replace the LM head with a scalar head, and train it to score responses.

class RewardModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base):
        super().__init__()
        self.base = base                                   # a pretrained transformer
        self.head = nn.Linear(base.cfg.d_model, 1, bias=False)

    def forward(self, ids, attn_mask):
        h = self.base.hidden_states(ids)                   # (B, T, C)
        last = attn_mask.sum(1) - 1                        # index of final real token
        return self.head(h[torch.arange(len(h)), last]).squeeze(-1)   # (B,)

The Bradley–Terry model

Humans give rankings, not scores. Bradley–Terry (1952) converts one into the other. It assumes each response has a latent quality r, and the probability of preferring yw over yl is a logistic function of their difference:

P(yw ≻ yl | x) = er(x,yw)er(x,yw) + er(x,yl) = σ(r(x,yw) − r(x,yl))

Maximum likelihood on that gives the reward-model loss:

ℒRM = − E(x,yw,yl) [ log σ(rφ(x,yw) − rφ(x,yl)) ] 
r_w = reward_model(chosen_ids,   chosen_mask)      # (B,)
r_l = reward_model(rejected_ids, rejected_mask)    # (B,)
loss = -F.logsigmoid(r_w - r_l).mean()
Worked example — the reward-model loss on four pairs

Now add 100 to both rewards in every row. The gap is unchanged, so every loss is identical. The model cannot tell 2-vs-1 from 102-vs-101.

That is why a reward score is meaningless on its own. Only differences are learned, so "this response scored 7.3" tells you nothing without something to compare against — and scores from two different reward models are not comparable at all. It is also why ln 2 = 0.6931 is your step-0 sanity check: an untrained reward model has no opinion, so the gap is 0. Remember this row — DPO's loss lands on exactly the same number for exactly the same reason (Chapter 20).

import math
for rw, rl in [(2,1), (5,1), (1,1), (1,2)]:
    p = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-(rw - rl)))
    print(rw, rl, round(p, 4), round(-math.log(p), 4))
# shifting both by any constant changes nothing:
assert abs((1/(1+math.exp(-(2-1)))) - (1/(1+math.exp(-(102-101))))) < 1e-12
Only differences are identified

The loss depends solely on r_w − r_l, so adding a constant to every reward changes nothing. The absolute scale is meaningless — a reward of 3.7 tells you nothing on its own. This is why reward models are evaluated by ranking accuracy on held-out pairs, never by their raw outputs. Remember this fact; it is what makes DPO work in Chapter 20.

19.4 · PPO

Now optimise the policy against that reward. The objective:

maxπθ   Ex∼D, y∼πθ [ rφ(x, y) ]  −  β · KL(πθ(y|x) ‖ πref(y|x)) maximise reward, but stay close to the SFT model
Why the KL term is non-negotiable

The reward model is an approximation of human preference, trained on a finite dataset. Optimise against it hard enough and the policy will find inputs where the approximation is badly wrong — outputs that score 10/10 and are nonsense. This is reward hacking, and without a KL constraint it happens essentially every time. Classic observed failure modes: responses that are absurdly long (length bias in the reward model), excessively hedged, or that repeat flattering phrases the reward model liked.

The clipped surrogate

PPO's contribution is a way to take large policy steps without destroying the policy:

ℒCLIP = E [ min( ρtÂt,   clip(ρt, 1−ε, 1+ε)Ât ) ] ρt = πθ(at|st) / πθold(at|st)  ·  Â = advantage  ·  ε ≈ 0.2

If the probability ratio moves more than ε away from 1, the objective is clipped and the gradient vanishes — so the update can't push the policy far from where the data was collected. That's what makes PPO stable enough to use.

What running PPO for RLHF actually requires

Four models in memory, two of them receiving gradients. Plus a generation loop inside the training loop, plus advantage estimation (GAE), plus a dozen hyperparameters that interact badly. RLHF via PPO is genuinely difficult to get working, and that difficulty is the entire reason DPO exists.

Reward hacking, essentially guaranteed. The reward model is a finite-sample approximation to human preference. Unconstrained optimisation against any approximation finds its failure modes — Goodhart's law, mechanised. The KL term keeps the policy in the region where the reward model was actually trained and is therefore trustworthy.
How many models are in memory during PPO-based RLHF, and which are trained?
2 — policy and reward model, both trained
4 — policy, reference, reward, value; policy and value are trained
3 — policy, reward, value; all trained
1 — only the policy
Four models, two trained. This is the practical burden that motivated DPO: memory, orchestration, and a large hyperparameter surface. For a 7B policy in bf16 you're holding roughly 4×14 GB of weights before optimizer state or activations.

19.5 · The data

{
  "prompt":   "Explain why the sky is blue to a 6-year-old.",
  "chosen":   "Sunlight looks white but is really made of all the colours...",
  "rejected": "Rayleigh scattering causes shorter wavelengths to scatter more..."
}

Note the rejected response isn't wrong — it's less appropriate for the request. Most preference data is about calibration and fit, not correctness.

  • Human preference — highest quality, expensive, slow, and annotator agreement is typically only 60–75%. That noise ceiling limits everything downstream.
  • AI feedback (RLAIF / Constitutional AI) — a strong model judges. Far cheaper and now dominant in practice; the risk is inheriting the judge's biases.
  • Verifiable signals — for maths and code, check the answer. No preference model needed at all. That's Chapter 21.
Known biases in preference data

Both human and AI annotators show measurable preference for longer responses, more confident phrasing, and formatted output (headers, bullet lists) — largely independent of actual quality. Models trained on it inherit all three. If your aligned model became verbose and started bulleting everything, that's not a bug in your training code; it's in your data. Length-debiasing the reward model, or using length-controlled evaluation, is now standard practice.

19.6 · Exercises

19.1SFT with correct maskingessential

Fine-tune a small model on 500 instruction pairs. Implement prompt masking, then verify it by decoding one example and printing which tokens have label == -100. Train once with masking and once without; compare generations from the same prompt.

What you'll observe

The unmasked model tends to continue by generating another user turn — it learned the whole conversation distribution, not the response distribution. It also converges more slowly on the part you care about. This is a five-minute experiment that permanently fixes the concept.

19.2Train a reward model and find its biasescode

Train a Bradley-Terry reward model on a public preference dataset. Then probe it:

  1. Ranking accuracy on a held-out split. (Above 70% is respectable; humans agree ~65–75% with each other.)
  2. Length bias: plot reward against response length. Fit a line. How much of the variance is length?
  3. Format bias: take 50 responses, reformat them with bullet points without changing content, and measure the reward change.
What you'll find

Length correlation is usually strong — often r > 0.3, sometimes much higher. Bulleting typically raises reward measurably. Now you can see, concretely, why RLHF'd models became verbose and list-happy around 2023–24. Once you can measure a bias you can correct it: subtract a length-fitted baseline from the reward and re-evaluate.

19.3Induce reward hacking on purposethinking

Using your reward model, run best-of-N sampling with N ∈ {1, 4, 16, 64, 256} and plot (a) mean reward and (b) your own quality rating of a sample of outputs, against N.

The expected divergence

Reward rises monotonically with N. Human-judged quality rises, peaks around N=16–64, then falls. Best-of-N is a KL-free optimiser against the reward model, so large N is exactly the regime where hacking appears. This is the cleanest demonstration of why the KL term exists — and it takes no RL machinery at all to reproduce.
