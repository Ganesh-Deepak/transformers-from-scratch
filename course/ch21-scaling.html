21 · Scaling Laws and Long Context

Two questions that determine how models get built: given a compute budget, how big should the model be? and how do you make a model trained at 4k tokens work at 128k? Both have surprisingly clean quantitative answers.

The whole chapter in five sentences

Loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters and data, and for a fixed training budget the useful rule is about 20 tokens per parameter, with parameters and data each growing roughly as √C (the published parametric fit says C0.45 and C0.55, and a 2024 replication says it was mis-fitted). Shipped models are trained far past that point because serving cost, not training cost, dominates, and the loss surface is flat. When fresh data runs out, repeating it is nearly free up to about 4 epochs and nearly worthless past a few dozen. Compute can also be spent at inference: with a verifier, sampling a small model many times can beat a much larger one. Long context is trained last, in stages, because at 128k attention is most of the compute, and an advertised window says nothing about how well it is used.

21.1 · Scaling laws

Kaplan et al. (2020) found something remarkable: test loss follows a smooth power law in model size, dataset size, and compute — over seven orders of magnitude, with no visible discontinuity.

L(N, D) = E + ANα + BDβ Hoffmann et al. 2022 fit: E = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28

Read the three terms:

  • E — the irreducible entropy of language. You cannot beat this no matter what.
  • A/Nα — the penalty for having too few parameters.
  • B/Dβ — the penalty for having seen too little data.

The Chinchilla correction

Kaplan's original analysis concluded you should scale model size much faster than data. GPT-3 followed that advice: 175B parameters on 300B tokens — about 1.7 tokens per parameter.

Hoffmann et al. (2022) redid the experiments with proper learning-rate schedules for each budget and found the opposite: N and D should scale roughly equally, at about 20 tokens per parameter. They proved it by training Chinchilla (70B on 1.4T tokens), which beat Gopher (280B on 300B tokens) across the board at the same training compute — a 4× smaller model, decisively better.

Two different "optima", and why they disagree

With C ≈ 6ND fixed, substitute D = C/(6N) into the loss and set the derivative in N to zero. The condition is that the two penalty terms are traded at equal marginal rates, α·A/Nα = β·B/Dβ, and it solves exactly (tfs.scaling.fitted_optimum):

N* = G · (C/6)a,   D* = (C/6) / N*,   a = β/(α+β),   b = α/(α+β),   G = (αA / (βB))1/(α+β) with the published α = 0.34, β = 0.28: N* ∝ C0.45, D* ∝ C0.55

So the fitted law does not say "both scale as √C". Because α ≠ β, data should grow a little faster than parameters, and the tokens-per-parameter ratio drifts upward with compute: about 50 at 1021 FLOPs, 78 at 1023, 122 at 1025, 191 at 1027. The famous 20 tokens per parameter comes from Hoffmann et al.'s other two methods (fitting the minimum of each IsoFLOP curve), which gave exponents close to 0.5 and 0.5. The two disagree, and Besiroglu et al. (2024) found why: re-fitting the paper's own data, the published parametric constants do not fit it well. Their refit (E = 1.82, A = 482, B = 2085, α = 0.348, β = 0.366) gives N* ∝ C0.51 and about 19 tokens per parameter at GPT-3's budget, consistent with the rule.

What to use: the 20-tokens-per-parameter rule (N* = √(C/120)) for sizing, and the fitted formula for the shape of the loss surface, remembering that its exponents are the least certain part of it. Every 4× of compute buys about 2× the parameters and 2× the data.

import math
E, A, B_, a, b = 1.69, 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28          # Hoffmann et al. 2022
def loss(N, D): return E + A / N**a + B_ / D**b

C = 3.14e23                                              # GPT-3's budget
N_opt = math.sqrt(C / 120); D_opt = C / (6 * N_opt)
print(round(loss(175e9, 300e9), 4))    # 2.0023  as built
print(round(loss(N_opt, D_opt), 4))    # 1.9610  20 tokens per parameter

from tfs.scaling import fitted_optimum
N_f, D_f, ea, eb = fitted_optimum(C)                     # 24.5e9, 2.14e12, 0.452, 0.548
print(round(loss(N_f, D_f), 4))        # 1.9543  the fitted law's own minimum
print(round(loss(8e9, 15e12), 4))      # 1.9485  Llama-3-8B
assert loss(N_opt, D_opt) < loss(175e9, 300e9)
Caveat These are fitted predictions, not measurements of those models. The constants come from Hoffmann et al.'s sweep on their data mixture; a different corpus moves them. The direction of the conclusion is robust, the third decimal place is not.

21.2 · When the data runs out: repeated epochs

Chinchilla assumes every token is fresh. At 20 tokens per parameter a 1T-parameter model wants 20T unique tokens, and over-trained small models want far more: Llama-3-8B's 15T, Qwen3-4B's 36T. The supply of good public text is finite (filtered web is a few tens of trillions of tokens, Chapter 11 §11.7), so the practical question becomes: what is a repeated token worth?

Muennighoff et al. (2023) trained hundreds of models with varying amounts of repetition and fitted a simple answer. With U unique tokens seen for R repetitions beyond the first (R = epochs − 1), the data is worth

D′ = U + U · R* · (1 − e−R/R*),    R* ≈ 15.4 each repetition is worth e^(−1/R*) of the previous one · D′ can never exceed U·(1 + R*) ≈ 16.4 U
Worked example — what epochs buy (tfs.scaling.effective_data, U = 1) 
epochs    effective data    worth per epoch
    1         1.00              1.00
    2         1.97              0.98
    4         3.73              0.93      <- "nearly as good as unique data"
    8         6.62              0.83
   16        10.58              0.66
   40        15.17              0.38
  100        16.36              0.16      <- the ceiling is 16.39: more passes add almost nothing

This is the paper's headline in numbers: up to about 4 epochs repetition is almost free (their 8.7B model at four epochs had only 0.5% higher validation loss than with unique data), around 16 epochs each pass is worth two-thirds of fresh data, and beyond that the returns collapse. Their models trained for 44 epochs started to diverge. Repetition also makes memorisation of the repeated text more likely, which matters separately from loss.

Two consequences for planning. Small high-value sources can be repeated a few times without regret, which is what makes Chapter 11's mixture upsampling viable: 4 epochs of maths is fine, 11 epochs of papers is already costing a third of its value. And in a data-constrained regime the compute-optimal model gets larger: when more data is not available, spending compute on more parameters (and fewer epochs) is the better trade. The paper also tried the obvious substitutes for scarce text, mixing in code and relaxing quality filters, and found that filling a substantial share of the data with code did not hurt the natural-language benchmarks they measured.

21.3 · Scaling laws for sparse and low-precision models

The Chinchilla law has two knobs, N and D. Two other choices change the curve, and each has its own scaling literature.

Mixture of Experts

An MoE separates total parameters from active ones (Chapter 20). Training cost follows active parameters (6·Nactive per token); what the model can store follows the total. Clark et al. (2022) fitted laws for routed models with the number of experts as a third variable and found that, at fixed compute per token, adding experts keeps lowering the loss with diminishing returns. Krajewski et al. (2024) added granularity (more, smaller experts) and found that the compute-optimal design uses finer-grained experts than the 8-expert designs of the time, which is the direction DeepSeek-V3's 256 experts took. The practical summary:

  • Compare MoE and dense at equal training FLOPs, i.e. equal active parameters × tokens. At equal compute an MoE reaches a lower loss; at equal total parameters it is worse than dense.
  • The Chinchilla ratio does not transfer directly. "20 tokens per parameter" was fitted for dense models; per active parameter, MoE runs are trained far longer (DeepSeek-V3: 14.8T tokens for 37B active, 400 per active parameter).
  • Memory and serving costs follow total parameters, which is the term scaling laws of training loss leave out (Chapter 20 §20.7).

Precision

Chapter 12 treats precision as an engineering choice that must not hurt the loss. Kumar et al. (2024) measured how it interacts with scale, over 465 training runs up to 1.7B parameters and 26B tokens. Two findings change how to think about it. Training in lower precision reduces the model's "effective parameter count": a model trained with low-precision weights behaves like a smaller model trained in high precision, which can be written into the law as an Neff < N. And post-training quantization hurts more the more data the model was trained on: heavily over-trained models (Llama-3-8B's 1,875 tokens per parameter) pack more into each weight and lose more when the weights are rounded, to the point where, for a model that will be quantized after training, extra pretraining data can make the deployed model worse. The paper's fits also suggest that training larger models in lower precision may be compute-optimal. These are recent results at small scale; treat the constants as provisional and the directions as the lesson.

21.4 · Test-time compute: scaling the answer, not the model

Everything so far spends compute on training. A second axis spends it at inference: sample several answers, or let the model reason longer, and pick the best result. For tasks with a checkable answer (unit tests, a numerical result, a proof checker) this scales remarkably well.

Repeated sampling: coverage

If one sample solves a problem with probability p and samples are independent, k samples solve it at least once with probability

coverage(k) = 1 − (1 − p)k p = 0.159: k = 1 → 0.159, k = 5 → 0.579, k = 10 → 0.823 (tfs.scaling.coverage)

Measured over a benchmark, coverage is estimated with the unbiased pass@k estimator of Chen et al. (2021): draw n ≥ k samples per problem, count the c correct ones, and compute 1 − C(n − c, k)/C(n, k) (tfs.scaling.pass_at_k; with n = 250 and c = 40 it gives exactly 0.16 at k = 1 and 0.831 at k = 10). Brown et al. (2024, "Large Language Monkeys") measured it at scale: on SWE-bench Lite, DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct solved 15.9% of issues with one sample and 56% with 250, beating the single-sample state of the art of 43%. Note that 56% is far below 1 − (1 − 0.159)250 ≈ 1: a benchmark mixes problems with very different p, and the ones with p ≈ 0 never get solved. That is why coverage grows roughly log-linearly in k over several orders of magnitude rather than saturating quickly.

Coverage is not accuracy

Coverage counts a problem as solved if any sample is right. That only becomes an answer if something can pick the right sample: a test suite, a checker, a verifier model. Without one, Brown et al. found that majority voting and reward-model selection plateau after a few hundred samples, far below coverage. Test-time scaling is strongest exactly where reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (Chapter 25) is strongest, and for the same reason.

Spending it well, and what it trades against

Snell et al. (2024) asked how to allocate a test-time budget: many independent samples, sequential revisions, or search guided by a process reward model. Choosing the strategy per prompt by estimated difficulty improved efficiency by more than 4× over best-of-N. In a FLOPs-matched comparison, a smaller model with extra test-time compute outperformed a 14× larger model on problems where the small model already had non-trivial success; on the hardest problems, pretraining compute was still the better buy. The cost model is simple: inference costs about 2·N FLOPs per generated token, so k samples of length L from a model of size N cost about 2·N·L·k, the same as one sample from a model k times larger. The reasoning models of 2025–26 (o1-style long chains of thought, Chapter 22) are this trade made inside a single sample: train the model to spend more tokens thinking, and let accuracy scale with the length it is allowed.

21.5 · Long context

A model trained at 4k tokens degrades badly at 32k (Chapter 7). RoPE's rotation angles go far outside the range it saw. Three families of fix:

Position Interpolation (PI)

# Squash positions into the trained range instead of extrapolating past it.
scale = trained_len / target_len          # e.g. 4096/32768 = 1/8
positions = torch.arange(T) * scale       # position 32767 → "4095.9"

Chen et al. (2023). Simple, works, needs a little fine-tuning. The cost: it compresses all frequencies equally, which crushes the high-frequency dimensions that encode fine local ordering — the model gets worse at distinguishing adjacent tokens.

NTK-aware scaling

# Instead of scaling positions, scale the RoPE BASE.
base_new = base * s ** (d / (d - 2))   # s = target_len / trained_len; d=128, s=8: 10000 → ~82,700

Interpolate the low-frequency (long-range) dimensions while leaving high-frequency ones nearly untouched. Better than PI, and often works with no fine-tuning at all. Llama 3's jump from base 10,000 to 500,000 is a training-time application of the same insight.

YaRN

Peng et al. (2023) — the current best of the three. It applies a per-dimension strategy based on each dimension's wavelength relative to the context:

  • Wavelength ≪ context → the dimension has seen full rotations; don't touch it (extrapolate).
  • Wavelength ≫ context → never completed a rotation; interpolate.
  • In between → blend smoothly.

Plus a temperature adjustment on the attention logits to compensate for the entropy change from longer sequences. YaRN reaches long contexts with roughly 10× less fine-tuning data than PI, and is what most context-extension work uses today.

Sliding-window attention

# Each token attends only to the previous W tokens.
mask = (i - j < W) & (j <= i)          # W = 4096 typically

Turns O(T²) into O(T·W). Information still propagates further than W because stacking layers extends the receptive field — n_layer × W in total, much like a CNN. Mistral 7B popularised this; Gemma 2/3 interleave local and global layers (alternating 1 : 1 in Gemma 2, 5 local : 1 global in Gemma 3) so most layers are cheap while a few retain full-range attention.

Attention sinks — why naive window eviction breaks models

Chapter 5's exercise found that many heads dump attention on token 0 regardless of content. Softmax forces every row to sum to 1, so a head with nothing relevant to attend to needs somewhere to put its mass — the first token becomes a "null" option. Evict token 0 from the cache and quality collapses. StreamingLLM's fix (Xiao et al. 2023) is trivial and effective: always keep the first ~4 tokens plus the sliding window. That one change enables stable generation over millions of tokens.

The staged recipe: how long context is actually trained

The techniques above say how to change RoPE. They do not say when, on what data, or for how long. No frontier model is pretrained at 128k from the start; every published recipe trains short and extends at the end, in stages. The reason is arithmetic. Training FLOPs per token are 6N plus an attention term that grows with the context length T, 12·L·dattn·T (Chapter 2):

Worked example — what fraction of training compute is attention? (tfs.scaling.attention_share) 
                          T = 8,192     T = 32,768    T = 131,072
Llama 3 405B (L=126, d=16384)   7.7%          25.0%         57.2%
Llama 3 8B   (L=32,  d=4096)   21.2%          51.8%         81.1%

At 128k, attention is more than half of every token's cost for the 405B model and four-fifths for the 8B, and "6N per token" underestimates the bill by 2.3× and 5.3×. Spending the whole pretraining run at 128k would multiply its cost for no benefit on the overwhelming majority of documents, which are short. Long documents are also scarce, activations per sequence grow with T (which is what context parallelism, Chapter 19 §19.6, exists for), and the model first needs to learn language before it can use long-range structure. So: pretrain at 4k–8k, and extend at the end.

Three things recur in every recipe. The data changes: the extension stages upsample genuinely long documents (books, code repositories, long papers) rather than concatenating short ones, since packed short documents teach nothing about using distant context. Short-context ability is protected: the mix keeps plenty of short data and the stage is judged by whether short benchmarks recover, not only by long ones. The batch is held in tokens, so the number of sequences per step falls as their length rises (DeepSeek's 1,920 → 480). The whole extension is a few percent of pretraining compute, which is why "we extended the context" is cheap to announce and the measurement of §21.6 is what tells you whether it worked.

21.6 · Measuring long context honestly

"Supports 1M tokens" is a claim about the config file

A model's advertised context length says what it will accept without erroring — not what it can use. Effective context is routinely a fraction of advertised context.

BenchmarkWhat it measuresDifficulty
Needle in a Haystackretrieve one planted fact from a long documenteasy — most models pass; near-saturated
RULERmulti-needle, aggregation, variable tracking, multi-hop QAhard — effective lengths often far below advertised
LongBench / ∞Benchrealistic long-document tasksrealistic
"Lost in the middle"accuracy vs. position of the relevant factreveals a U-shaped curve: strong at the ends, weak in the middle

The last one is the most practically useful finding: information at the beginning and end of a long context is used far more reliably than information in the middle. If you're building a RAG system, put the most important retrieved chunks at the edges — it measurably improves answers.

21.1Fit your own scaling lawcode

Train tiny models at 5 sizes (roughly 0.1M, 0.3M, 1M, 3M, 10M non-embedding params) on Tiny Shakespeare for a fixed number of tokens each. Plot final loss vs N on log-log axes and fit a power law.

  1. What exponent do you get? Is it near 0.34?
  2. Extrapolate to 100M. Then train a 100M model and check.
What to expect

You'll get a clean power law over the small range, but the exponent will likely differ from 0.34 — Chinchilla's constants are fitted on a specific data distribution at much larger scale. The form generalises; the constants don't. Your extrapolation will also break down as you approach the dataset's irreducible entropy, since 1 MB of Shakespeare is exhausted long before 100M parameters are.

Take your Chapter 11 model trained at max_T=128. Evaluate perplexity at sequence lengths 128, 256, 512, 1024 with: (a) no change, (b) position interpolation, (c) NTK-aware base scaling, (d) YaRN. Plot perplexity vs length for all four.

Expected shape

(a) explodes immediately past 128. (b) degrades gracefully but is worse than (a) at 128 — the price of compressing frequencies you didn't need to compress. (c) and (d) stay flat furthest, with YaRN best at the longest lengths. That "PI is worse at the original length" crossover is the key observation and the reason the field moved past PI.

21.3Run your own needle testapplied

Build a needle-in-a-haystack harness: insert "The secret code is 7391." at depth d ∈ {0%, 10%, …, 100%} into filler text of length L ∈ {1k, 2k, 4k, 8k, 16k}, then ask the model for the code. Build a heatmap of accuracy over (L, d) for any local model.

What you'll find

A characteristic pattern: strong at depth 0% and 100%, weakest around 30–70% — the "lost in the middle" effect — with the weak band widening as L grows. Then try the harder version: two needles that must be combined. Accuracy usually falls off a cliff. Single-needle retrieval is a much easier task than reasoning over long context, which is why RULER exists and why NIAH scores are a poor basis for choosing a model.

You have 1024 FLOPs and 3T tokens of data you trust.

  1. The 20-token rule's N and D. How many epochs of your data would that need?
  2. The effective data of those epochs (tfs.scaling.effective_data). Is the plan still sensible?
  3. Alternatively, train for 4 epochs (12T tokens seen). What model size does the compute budget then allow?
Answers
rule of 20:   N = √(1e24 / 120) = 91.3B,   D = 1.83T tokens   -> 0.6 epochs: no repetition needed
4 epochs:     D = 12T seen, effective 3.73 × 3T = 11.2T;  N = 1e24 / (6 × 12e12) = 13.9B

At this budget the data is not the constraint for a compute-optimal model; it becomes one only if you over-train a small model for cheap serving. Then 4 epochs of 3T costs you about 7% of its value (11.2T effective of 12T seen), which is usually acceptable; 16 epochs would cost a third.

21.5Sampling versus a bigger modelthinking

A 7B model solves a problem class with p = 0.30 per sample and a unit-test verifier is available. A 70B model solves it with p = 0.60. Inference costs about 2N FLOPs per token, and answers are the same length.

  1. Coverage of the 7B model with 10 samples, and its cost relative to one 70B sample.
  2. When would you still prefer the 70B model?
Answers

Coverage(10) = 1 − 0.710 = 0.972, at 10 × 7B = the same FLOPs as one 70B sample (which succeeds 60% of the time). With a verifier, the small model wins clearly.

Prefer the 70B when there is no reliable verifier (selection then plateaus well below coverage), when latency matters more than throughput (ten samples in parallel need ten times the memory bandwidth budget), or when p for the small model is near zero, where no number of samples helps.

Using tfs.scaling.attention_share and the 6N + 12·L·d·T accounting, compute the FLOPs of DeepSeek-V3-style extension stages (62.9B tokens at 32K, then 62.9B at 128K) for an 8B dense model (L = 32, d = 4096). Compare with pretraining it on 15T tokens at 8K. Then plot attention share against T from 1K to 1M for both 8B and 405B.

What you should find

Per token, a 128K step costs about 4× an 8K step for the 8B model, so the two extension stages together cost about the FLOPs of 0.37T tokens at 8K: 2.4% of the 15T-token pretraining. The plot crosses 50% attention near 30K tokens for the 8B and near 100K for the 405B: the larger the model, the longer the context before attention dominates, because 6N grows with d² and the attention term only with d.
