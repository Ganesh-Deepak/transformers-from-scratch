26 · Evaluating LLMs

Every chapter so far ends with a claim that something got better: a lower loss, a higher win rate, more problems solved. This chapter is about whether you should believe it. An evaluation is a measurement, and like any measurement it has a resolution, a bias and ways to be fooled. Most published benchmark gaps are smaller than the benchmark can detect. Many benchmarks are partly in the training data. And the most popular judges prefer long answers. None of that makes evaluation hopeless. It makes it a discipline with arithmetic, and the arithmetic is short.

The whole chapter in six sentences

An eval is a dataset, a prompt format, a decoding setup, a scorer and an aggregate, and changing any one of them changes the number. A benchmark of n items cannot detect differences much smaller than 2.8·√(2p(1−p)/n): 13 points on GPQA Diamond, 1.2 on MMLU. Compare two models on the same items, because the paired difference is far less noisy than the two scores are. Sampled metrics need unbiased estimators. pass@k from n samples is 1 − C(n−c, k)/C(n, k), not 1 − (1 − c/n)k. Model judges are biased toward the first answer, the longer answer and their own answers, and each bias has a standard correction. Public benchmarks leak into training data and saturate, so the eval that decides what you ship is a private one that you built and keep.

26.1 · What an eval actually is

"Model X scores 71% on GPQA" sounds like a property of model X. It is a property of a whole pipeline, and the pipeline has five parts:

PartWhat you chooseHow much it can move the number
Datasetwhich items, which split, which versiona revised benchmark is a different benchmark
Prompt formatzero- or few-shot, chat template, instructions, answer formatseveral points on multiple-choice benchmarks (Sclar et al. 2023 measured spreads of tens of points from formatting alone on small models)
Decodinggreedy or sampled, temperature, max tokens, thinking budget (Ch 13)large for reasoning models, where a longer budget is literally more computation
Scorerexact match, normalised match, unit tests, a judge model"Paris." vs "Paris" is a 0 or a 1
Aggregatemean accuracy, pass@k, win rate, Elo; over which itemspass@1 and pass@16 are different questions

The practical consequence: a number can be compared only with numbers produced by the same pipeline. In 2023 the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard found that three popular implementations of MMLU, all honest, gave the same model scores far enough apart to reorder the leaderboard. One scored the probability of the answer letter, one the probability of the full answer text, and one generated text and parsed it. When you read a model card, look for the harness and the settings before you look at the score.

Two ways to score a multiple-choice question

Log-likelihood scoring never lets the model generate. For each option it computes the log-probability of the option text (or its letter) after the question, using exactly the sequence_logp of Chapter 24, and picks the largest. It is cheap and deterministic, and it works on base models that cannot follow an instruction. Generative scoring lets the model answer freely, then parses the answer. It measures what a user would see, including whether the model can follow the format, and it is the only option for chain-of-thought and reasoning models. The two disagree most on small or base models. That is why a base-model leaderboard and a chat-model leaderboard cannot be swapped for each other.

Length-normalised log-likelihood is a choice, not a fix

Summed log-probabilities favour short options, because every extra token adds a negative number. So harnesses often divide by the number of tokens or characters. That is the same choice between sum and average as DPO against SimPO (Ch 24, Ch 25), and it changes which option wins. Whichever you use, use it for every model you compare.

Saturation, and why it happens faster every year

MMLU was released in 2020, when GPT-3 scored 43.9% on it. Within four years frontier models were near 90%, where the remaining errors are a mix of truly hard items and items whose answer key is wrong. A benchmark is saturated when the differences between strong models are smaller than its noise and its error rate. It still detects a broken model, but it can no longer rank good ones. Three forces shorten a benchmark's useful life: models improve, the benchmark leaks into training data, and labs optimise for the numbers that appear on their model cards. Goodhart's law applies to benchmarks exactly as it applied to the reward model in Chapter 23: a measure that is optimised against stops measuring.

A noise ceiling you cannot train past

If 6.5% of a benchmark's answer keys are wrong, a model that is right about everything scores at most 93.5%, and a model at 95% is partly matching the mistakes. Near the top of a benchmark, the leaderboard measures agreement with the answer key, not correctness.

Two papers report the same base model at 63.1% and 66.8% on MMLU. Neither has a bug. What is the most likely explanation?
One of them used a contaminated checkpoint
Random seed variance in greedy decoding
Different pipelines: few-shot count, prompt format, and whether the letter or the full answer was scored by log-likelihood
MMLU has two different test sets
The pipeline is part of the number. Greedy decoding is deterministic, and the test set is the same. Formatting and scoring choices often move multiple-choice scores by several points, which is why numbers can be compared only within one harness.
Why can log-likelihood scoring evaluate a base model that generative scoring cannot?
It never asks the model to follow an answer format; it only compares the probabilities it assigns to each option
It uses a larger context window
Base models cannot generate text
It averages over many samples
No generation, so no format to follow. A base model asked a question may write five more questions (Ch 23). Comparing the log-probability of each option skips instruction-following entirely. That is also why it can overstate what a user would experience.
A benchmark has about 6.5% wrong answer keys. Model A scores 94.8%, model B 93.2%. What can you conclude?
A is clearly better
B is clearly better, because A must be memorising
Both are at the ceiling, so they are equal
Very little: the gap is inside the region where the score partly measures agreement with wrong labels
Above the noise ceiling, ranking is unreliable. A model right about everything would score about 93.5%. A higher score means matching some wrong keys, which can come from memorising the benchmark or from luck. You need a cleaner benchmark, or a re-labelled subset, to tell them apart.

26.3 · Contamination: has the model seen the test?

A pretraining corpus of 15 trillion tokens scraped from the web contains most public benchmarks. The questions are on GitHub, in blog posts, and in forum threads about them. A model that has seen an item can answer it from memory instead of with the skill the benchmark meant to measure. This is contamination, and it comes in degrees: the exact item with its answer, the item without the answer, a paraphrase, or just heavy exposure to the same template.

Detecting it when you have the training data: n-gram overlap

If you can search the corpus, check whether long word sequences from each test item appear in it. GPT-3's paper used 13-gram overlap, on the idea that a 13-word sequence almost never repeats by chance. The function ngram_overlap reports the fraction of an item's n-grams found in the corpus:

from tfs.evals import ngrams, ngram_overlap
corpus = "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog and then the fox runs into the forest to find food"
cg = ngrams(corpus.split(), 5)                               # every 5-gram in the corpus
ngram_overlap("a quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog today", cg, 5)    # 0.667: 4 of its 6 5-grams
ngram_overlap("the fox jumps over the lazy cat and runs away",   cg, 5)    # 0.167: 1 of 6

At corpus scale you hash the n-grams into a Bloom filter or a suffix array. Two warnings. Overlap misses paraphrases and translations entirely. And the threshold is a choice. The Llama 2 report flags an item as contaminated above a chosen fraction of matching tokens, and different fractions give different "clean" subsets.

Detecting it from outside: membership signals

For a closed model you have only its outputs and, sometimes, its log-probabilities. Text a model was trained on surprises it unusually little, and the signal is clearest in the hardest tokens. A memorised passage has few very unlikely tokens, while an unseen one has several. Min-K% Prob (Shi et al. 2023) averages the log-probability of the k% least likely tokens:

Worked example — Min-K% Prob on two 10-token passages, k = 20%
1
Per-token log-probs the model assigns to each passage: 
seen     -0.2 -0.5 -0.1 -1.1 -0.3 -0.4 -0.9 -0.2 -0.6 -0.3     mean -0.46

seen     -1.1, -0.9   ->  -1.0

The gap widens from 1.18 to 3.5 nats. The mean is dominated by easy tokens that both passages share ("the", "of"), and the least likely tokens are where memorisation shows. In practice you set a threshold on text you know was or was not in training. The method gives a statistical signal per benchmark, not a verdict per item.

The two tests that settle it: a fresh copy, and the calendar

The most convincing contamination test does not inspect the model at all. Write a new version of the benchmark with the same difficulty and see whether scores drop. GSM1k (Zhang et al. 2024) did this for GSM8K: 1,250 new grade-school problems written by people, with no model involved. Several model families scored up to 8 points lower on the new problems than on the old ones, while others showed no drop. The gap tracked how likely each model was to generate GSM8K text word for word. The second test is the calendar. LiveCodeBench tags every problem with its contest date, so you can score a model only on problems published after its training cutoff. If the score drops sharply at the cutoff date, the earlier score came partly from memory.

Contamination you cause yourself

The easiest contamination to create is your own. Synthetic SFT data generated by a strong model (Ch 23) can reproduce benchmark items the generator memorised. A "decontaminated" dataset scraped after a benchmark became popular contains its discussion threads. Before training, deduplicate every training set you build against every eval you report, with an n-gram filter. BIG-bench put a canary string (a unique GUID) in its files so that corpus builders can find and drop them, so check for canaries too.

26.4 · Scoring: exact match, executable checks, and model judges

Once the model has answered, something has to decide whether the answer is right. In order of how much you should trust them:

  1. Execution. Run the code against tests, check the final database state, verify the proof. It is the strongest scorer when it exists, with the weaknesses of the verifiers in Chapter 25: tests can be incomplete, and a model can pass them without solving the problem.
  2. Normalised exact match. Compare the extracted answer to the gold answer after normalisation. SQuAD's normalisation, in normalize_answer, lower-cases, strips punctuation and the articles a/an/the, and collapses whitespace.
  3. Equivalence checks. For maths, 1/2, 0.5 and the same fraction written in LaTeX are one answer. Parse both sides and compare them symbolically.
  4. Token F1. Partial credit by word overlap, for extractive questions.
  5. A model judge. For anything open-ended. Strong but biased; see section 26.7.
from tfs.evals import exact_match, token_f1, majority_vote
exact_match("The Eiffel Tower.", "Eiffel Tower")                   # 1  after normalisation
exact_match("The Eiffel Tower.", "Eiffel Tower", normalize=False)  # 0  raw string compare
token_f1("the tower in Paris", "Eiffel Tower")                     # 0.4: P = 1/3, R = 1/2
majority_vote(["42", "41", "42.", "forty-two", "42"])              # "42": 3 of 5 after normalising

The F1 of 0.4 is worth reading slowly. After normalisation the prediction is tower in paris (3 words) and the gold is eiffel tower (2 words). One word is shared, so precision is 1/3, recall 1/2, and F1 = 2·(1/3)(1/2)/(1/3 + 1/2) = 0.4. Note that "forty-two" is not matched to "42". Normalisation is not understanding, and model judges fill that gap.

Answer extraction is where most eval bugs live

Reasoning models write long derivations and then an answer, sometimes several candidate answers. A regex that takes the first number instead of the last, or misses \boxed{} written with a space, quietly scores correct answers as wrong. Before trusting any new eval, read 20 scored outputs by hand and count how many the scorer got wrong. This is the eval version of Chapter 23's "print a decoded example with the masked positions marked".

26.5 · How precise is a benchmark? Error bars from first principles

Treat the benchmark's items as a sample from a much larger population of questions that test the same skill (Miller 2024 calls it the super-population). Each item is a coin flip with the model's true accuracy p. The accuracy measured over n independent items then has a standard error:

SE = √( p(1 − p) / n )      95% interval ≈ p ± 1.96 · SE the binomial standard error · the item set, not the model, is the random variable

Most leaderboard gaps between frontier models on small benchmarks are smaller than the benchmark can see. The models are not necessarily equal. This measurement simply cannot tell. Read every score as an interval, and ask how many items stood behind it.

Pair the comparison: the single biggest improvement you can make

The formula above treats the two models' scores as independent. They are not. Both models answered the same items, and items vary hugely in difficulty. Most items are either easy for both or hard for both, and they add nothing to the difference. Compute the difference per item, di = scoreB,i − scoreA,i (which is −1, 0 or +1), and put the error bar on the mean of d. Its variance is the probability that the models disagree, minus the squared gap. That is usually far smaller than the sum of the two models' variances.

The bootstrap gives the same answer without formulas. Resample the items with replacement many times, recompute the statistic each time, and read the interval off the spread. The one rule is to resample items, and resample the pair together.

def bootstrap_ci(scores, n_boot=10_000, alpha=0.05, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n = len(scores)
    means = sorted(sum(scores[rng.randrange(n)] for _ in range(n)) / n   # resample ITEMS
                   for _ in range(n_boot))
    return means[int(alpha / 2 * n_boot)], means[int((1 - alpha / 2) * n_boot) - 1]

def paired_bootstrap_diff(a, b, **kw):            # a, b: per-item scores on the SAME items
    return bootstrap_ci([y - x for x, y in zip(a, b)], **kw)

If your eval tool reports two separate confidence intervals and you check whether they overlap, you are doing the unpaired analysis, and you will miss real improvements.

How many items do you need to see a 2-point gain?

Turn the question around: you expect a change to move accuracy from 70% to 72%. How large must the eval be? With independent samples, the two-proportion formula gives

n = (zα/2 + zβ)2 · ( p1q1 + p2q2 ) / δ2 = 2.802 × (0.21 + 0.2016) / 0.022 = 8,077 items per model α = 0.05 two-sided, power 0.8, so zα/2 + zβ = 1.960 + 0.842 = 2.802 · q = 1 − p

Paired, the variance per item is the disagreement rate minus δ2. If the two models disagree on 10% of items (typical for two checkpoints of one model):

n = 2.802 × (0.10 − 0.022) / 0.022 = 1,955 items n_items_unpaired(0.70, 0.72) == 8077 · n_items_paired(0.10, 0.02) == 1955

Pairing cuts the eval you need by a factor of four. And both numbers are far larger than GPQA Diamond (198) or AIME (30). For small benchmarks the honest options are these. Sample several answers per item and average them (this reduces the within-item noise of a sampled model, though not the item-sampling noise). Or pool related benchmarks. Or report the interval and stop there.

Two refinements Miller (2024) recommends

Clustered standard errors. When items come in groups (several questions on one reading passage, several tests of one function), they are not independent, and the plain SE is too small. Compute the SE over clusters instead. On reading-comprehension benchmarks it can be several times larger. Separate the two sources of noise. A sampled model's score on one item is itself random. Averaging k samples per item shrinks that part by 1/k, but only more items shrink the other part.

26.6 · Sampled metrics: pass@k, derived

For code and maths we often care whether the model can produce a correct answer in k attempts: pass@k, the probability that at least one of k samples is correct. If the model's per-sample success rate on a problem is p, then

pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)k at least one success in k independent tries

We do not know p. The obvious estimate draws k samples and checks whether any is right, but that is very noisy. A better way is to draw n ≥ k samples once and count the c correct ones. Then ask what fraction of all the ways of choosing k of the n samples contains at least one correct sample.

pass@k estimate = 1 − C(n − c, k)C(n, k) C(n−c, k) counts the k-subsets made only of wrong samples · Chen et al. 2021 (HumanEval)

Why this is unbiased

Think of the n samples as a random sample, and then take a random k-subset of them. The k-subset is itself a set of k independent samples from the model, so the probability that it contains no correct sample is exactly (1 − p)k on average. The estimator computes that probability for the k-subset exactly, by counting, instead of drawing one at random. So averaging over the randomness of which c you happened to get gives the true value. The test suite checks this without sampling at all. It sums the estimator over every c, weighted by the binomial probability of seeing c, and gets 1 − (1 − p)k to nine decimal places.

The naive plug-in estimate 1 − (1 − c/n)k is biased low. The function 1 − (1 − p)k is concave in p, so by Jensen's inequality its average over a noisy estimate of p is below its value at the true p.

Worked example — n = 10 samples, c = 3 correct
1
pass@1 = 1 − C(7,1)/C(10,1) = 1 − 7/10 = 0.300. For k = 1 the estimator is just c/n.
2
pass@5 = 1 − C(7,5)/C(10,5) = 1 − 21/252 = 0.9167. Of the 252 ways to pick 5 of the 10 samples, only 21 avoid all three correct ones.
3
The naive estimate 1 − 0.75 = 0.8319. Averaged over everything you could have observed when the true p is 0.3, the naive estimator gives 0.760 instead of the true 0.832, an 8-point underestimate. The unbiased one gives 0.832 exactly.

Compute it as a product. C(n, k) overflows for n = 200; the HumanEval paper's form, 1 − Πi = n−c+1..n (1 − k/i), never does, and is what pass_at_k uses.

def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    if n - c < k:                      # every k-subset contains a correct sample
        return 1.0
    prod = 1.0
    for i in range(n - c + 1, n + 1):
        prod *= 1 - k / i              # = C(n-c, k) / C(n, k), one factor at a time
    return 1 - prod                    # average this over problems for the benchmark score

pass@k, maj@k and pass^k answer different questions

MetricQuestion it answersWho needs it
pass@1 (averaged over n samples)how often is a single answer right?anyone serving one answer; the default
pass@kcan it get there at all with k tries and a perfect checker?RL (Ch 25: a prompt with pass@8 = 0 gives no GRPO signal), best-of-n with a verifier
maj@k / cons@kis the most common of k answers right?self-consistency without a verifier
pass^kdoes it succeed on all of k tries?agents, where a user retries the same task (26.8)
pass@1 for reasoning models is a mean over many samples

Reasoning models are evaluated with sampling, not greedy decoding (greedy decoding makes long chains of thought loop). DeepSeek-R1's report, for example, samples at temperature 0.6 and reports pass@1 as the average correctness over many samples per problem (up to 64), alongside cons@64. On a 30-problem AIME, this averaging is what makes the number usable at all. It removes the sampling noise of each problem, leaving only the (still large) noise of having just 30 problems.

26.7 · Model judges: LLM-as-judge and its biases

Open-ended answers ("explain this", "write that") have no gold answer to match. Asking people is the gold standard, but it is slow. Asking a strong model is fast. Zheng et al. (2023) showed that GPT-4 as a judge agreed with human preferences over 80% of the time on MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena data, about as often as humans agree with each other. Since then, model judges have graded most open-ended evals. They have three well-measured biases.

Position bias, and the swap test

Shown two answers, judges favour one slot, usually the first. In the MT-Bench study even GPT-4 gave the same verdict after the two answers were swapped in only about two-thirds of cases. The fix is mechanical: judge every pair in both orders, and count a win only if it survives the swap.

def debias_pairwise(v_ab, v_ba):          # v_ab: model shown FIRST; v_ba: model shown SECOND
    wins_ab = v_ab == "first"
    wins_ba = v_ba == "second"
    if wins_ab and wins_ba:         return "win"
    if not wins_ab and not wins_ba: return "loss"
    return "tie"                             # the verdict followed the slot, not the answer

A judge that says "first" both times is not judging the answers at all, and the swap turns that into a tie instead of a false win. Report the consistency rate too, the fraction of pairs on which the two orders agree. It is a free measure of how much to trust the judge on this task.

Length bias, and length-controlled win rates

Judges, like the human annotators of Chapter 23, prefer longer answers. A model can raise its win rate by being wordy without being better. In 2023 several did, which made the AlpacaEval leaderboard easy to game. Length-controlled AlpacaEval (Dubois et al. 2024) fixes this with a regression. It models the judge's preference as a function of a quality term and a length term, fits it, and reports the win rate with the length term set to zero. In other words: how often would this model win if its answers were the same length as the baseline's?

P(wini) = σ( θ + φ · tanh( Δleni / sd ) )      LC win rate = σ(θ) Δlen = model answer length − baseline answer length · the real metric adds a per-instruction difficulty term
Worked example — a model that wins only by being long

805 instructions (AlpacaEval's size). The model's answers average 417 tokens, the baseline's 297. The simulated judge in the test uses only length: P(win) = σ(1.5 · tanh(Δlen/sd)), quality term zero.

1
Raw win rate: the model wins 499 of 805 = 62.0%. On a leaderboard it looks clearly better than the baseline.
2
Fit the regression (length_controlled_winrate, Newton's method on two parameters): φ = 1.54 (the true value was 1.5) and θ = −0.104.
3
LC win rate = σ(−0.104) = 47.4%, within noise of the true 50%.

Twelve points of "quality" were length. On real data, Dubois et al. report that length control raised the Spearman correlation of AlpacaEval with Chatbot Arena rankings from 0.94 to 0.98. It also made the metric much harder to game by asking for longer answers. Chatbot Arena itself later added a "style control" option that removes the effect of length and markdown formatting in the same way.

Self-preference, and the rest

Judges rate their own outputs, and those of similar models, higher than other judges do. Panickssery et al. (2024) found that the more reliably a model can recognise its own text, the more it favours it. The fixes are to use a judge from a different family than the models being compared, or a panel of judges from several families, and to check the judge against human labels on your task. Other documented biases: judges are swayed by confident tone, by formatting (headers, bullets), and by an answer that agrees with a reference answer shown in the prompt, even when the reference is wrong.

Rubrics beat vibes

A judge asked "which is better?" brings its own taste. A judge asked "does the answer state the dosage? does it cite the guideline? is it under 150 words?" is closer to an exact-match scorer, and its biases have less room to act. For anything you will ship, write the rubric first, have the judge score each criterion separately, and check the judge against a few hundred examples labelled by people (26.9).

Arena ratings are Bradley-Terry

Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al. 2024) collects human votes on anonymous pairs and fits exactly the Bradley-Terry model of Chapter 23, with each model's strength in place of a reward, reported on the Elo scale:

P(A beats B) = 1 / ( 1 + 10−(RA − RB)/400 ) 100 points → 64.0% · 30 points → 54.3% · 400 points → 90.9%

Two models 30 points apart differ by four points of head-to-head win rate. That is why Arena publishes bootstrap confidence intervals on each rating. Models whose intervals overlap are not reliably ordered.

26.8 · Evaluating reasoning models and agents

Reasoning: the budget is part of the result

A reasoning model's accuracy rises with the number of tokens it is allowed to think. A score without its thinking budget (maximum tokens, or the effort setting) is like a latency number without the batch size. For each eval, report the sampling temperature, the maximum output length, the number of samples averaged per item, and the mean number of tokens actually generated. The last one is also the cost. And check how many responses hit the length limit. A cut-off chain of thought scores zero for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the model could solve the problem.

Agents: the environment is part of the model

An agent eval runs a model inside a loop with tools (a shell, a browser, an API) for many turns, and scores the final state. Did the repository's tests pass (SWE-bench)? Is the airline database in the right state (τ-bench)? Did the terminal task's checker succeed (Terminal-Bench)? Three things set them apart from question-answering evals:

  • The scaffold matters as much as the model. The same model can score very differently on SWE-bench with different tool sets, prompts and retry policies. A score is a score for a model and a harness.
  • Environments are noisy. Network calls time out, simulated users vary, tests are flaky. Run each task several times.
  • Reliability is the product requirement. A user who retries a task wants it to work every time. τ-bench's pass^k is the probability that all k trials of a task succeed. Its unbiased estimate from n trials with c successes is C(c, k)/C(n, k), and for a per-trial success rate p it equals pk.
Worked example — the reliability cliff
1
An agent succeeds on a task 60% of the time. pass@1 = 0.60. On a leaderboard that is a decent score.
2
pass^8 = 0.68 = 0.0168. The chance that eight users, each trying this task once, are all served correctly is under 2%.
3
From data, n = 10 trials with c = 8 successes gives pass^4 = C(8,4)/C(10,4) = 70/210 = 0.333, where the plug-in 0.84 would say 0.410.

pass@k rises with k, and pass^k falls with it. One measures capability with retries and a checker, and the other measures dependability. People buy agents for the second.

Grade agents on outcomes (the end state) instead of on the transcript wherever you can. A transcript judge rewards steps that look plausible, while the state check rewards the work being done. And report cost per task alongside success rate. An agent that solves 5% more tasks with four times the tokens may be the wrong trade.

26.9 · Building a private eval set

Public benchmarks tell you how a model compares with others on tasks somebody else chose. The decision in front of you needs an eval of your task that nobody has trained on: which checkpoint to ship, whether a fine-tune helped, or whether a prompt change broke something. Building one is unglamorous work, and it is usually the most valuable evaluation work a team does.

  1. Name the decision. "Is checkpoint B better than A for support replies?" sets what to measure and how big a difference matters.
  2. Sample real inputs from the traffic you will serve, spread over the slices you care about (languages, product areas, difficulty). Synthetic inputs can add to them but cannot replace them.
  3. Write a rubric of checkable criteria before you look at model outputs, so the rubric is not fitted to one model's style.
  4. Label with two people on a shared subset and measure their agreement with Cohen's κ. If people cannot agree, no judge model will either, and the criterion needs rewriting.
  5. Size it with section 26.5: decide the smallest difference you need to see, and compute the items.
  6. Keep it private, version it, and keep it out of every training set (n-gram filter, canary string). Add the failures you find in production as new items, and never delete the old ones. That is your regression suite.
  7. Calibrate any judge against the human labels, and re-check whenever you change the judge model.
Worked example — 80% agreement that is only κ = 0.52

Two annotators label 20 responses as acceptable (Y) or not (N):

annotator 1   Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y N N N N N N     14 Y, 6 N
annotator 2   Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y Y N N Y Y N N N N     14 Y, 6 N
1
Observed agreement po = 16/20 = 0.80.
2
Agreement expected by chance from the label rates alone: pe = 0.7 × 0.7 + 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.58.
3
κ = (po − pe)/(1 − pe) = 0.22/0.42 = 0.524.

When most answers are acceptable, two annotators agree a lot by accident. κ = 0.52 is "moderate" agreement. It is good enough to start, but not good enough to detect small differences between models. The usual fix is a sharper rubric, not more annotators.

The eval-set lifecycle

Every time you look at a failure and change the model, the prompt or the data because of it, that item becomes slightly less of a test. Over months a private eval drifts toward being a training set. Keep a held-out part that nobody looks at item by item, and use it only for the final decision, just as you would never tune hyperparameters on the test split.

26.10 · The mental model to keep

  • A score is a pipeline's output. Dataset, format, decoding, scorer, aggregate. Compare only like with like.
  • Every score has an error bar of √(p(1−p)/n). Small benchmarks resolve only large gaps: ±6.4 points on GPQA Diamond, ±18 on one year of AIME.
  • Pair your comparisons. Use the same items, per-item differences, and a bootstrap over items. It needs a quarter of the data.
  • Use unbiased estimators for sampled metrics. pass@k = 1 − C(n−c,k)/C(n,k); pass^k = C(c,k)/C(n,k).
  • Judges are biased in known directions. Swap positions, control for length, judge across families, and check against people.
  • Public benchmarks leak and saturate. The eval that decides what you ship is private, sized by a power calculation, and kept out of training.

26.11 · Exercises

26.1Predict, then measure: how much does the prompt format move a score?essential

Take 300 items of a multiple-choice benchmark and any small open model. Score it four ways: answer-letter log-likelihood, full-answer log-likelihood (summed), full-answer log-likelihood (per-token average), and generation with answer parsing. Before running, write down which you expect to be highest and by how much.

  1. Report the four accuracies with 95% intervals.
  2. Compute the paired difference between the two log-likelihood variants. Is it significant?
  3. For generation, read 20 outputs the parser marked wrong. How many were actually right?
What to look for

Expect the four numbers to differ by more than their separate error bars on a small model. Expect the choice between sum and average to flip the winner on a noticeable share of items whose options differ in length. The parser check usually finds a few correct answers marked wrong. That is a bug in the eval, not in the model, and it is the most common one.

26.2Implement pass@k and prove it is unbiasedcode

Write pass_at_k(n, c, k) in the product form. Then, for true rates p ∈ {0.05, 0.3, 0.7} and k ∈ {1, 5, 10} with n = 20:

  1. Simulate 100,000 draws of c ~ Binomial(20, p) and average your estimator and the naive one.
  2. Compare both with 1 − (1 − p)k. Where is the naive bias largest?
  3. Replace the simulation with the exact sum tfs.evals.expected_estimator uses, and confirm the unbiased one matches to 1e-9.
Answer to 2

The bias is largest for small p and large k, where 1 − (1 − p)k is most curved and a single lucky or unlucky sample moves p̂ the most. For k = 1 the two estimators are identical (both are c/n), so there is no bias at all.

26.3Cost an evaluation before you run itthinking

You evaluate a reasoning model on one year of AIME with 64 samples per problem, averaging 15,000 output tokens per sample.

  1. Total output tokens. At an assumed $10 per million output tokens, what does one run cost?
  2. On your own server generating 2,000 tokens/s in aggregate, how long does it take?
  3. After all that, what is the 95% interval on pass@1 if the model scores 50%? What did the 64 samples buy you, and what did they not?
Answers
1. 30 × 64 × 15,000 = 28,800,000 tokens  →  28.8 × $10 = $288
2. 28,800,000 / 2,000 = 14,400 s = 4.0 hours
3. still about ±17.9 points: SE = √(0.5 × 0.5 / 30) = 0.091

The 64 samples remove the within-problem sampling noise, so pass@1 is a stable property of these 30 problems. They do nothing about there being only 30 problems. To narrow the interval you need more problems (pool several years, or add MATH-level items), not more samples.

26.4Measure a judge's position and length biascode

Pick 100 instructions and two models. Ask a judge model to compare each pair in both orders.

  1. Report the consistency rate, and the fraction of inconsistent verdicts that favoured slot 1.
  2. Pad one model's answers with a polite but empty closing paragraph (no new content) and re-judge. How much does its win rate move?
  3. Fit length_controlled_winrate on the padded run. Does the LC win rate recover the unpadded result?
What you'll see

Expect consistency well below 100%, with the inconsistencies leaning toward one slot. Padding usually raises the win rate even though it adds nothing, which is the length bias in its purest form, and the LC estimate should move back toward the unpadded number. If padding lowers the win rate, your judge prompt penalises wordiness. That is a bias in the other direction, and also worth knowing about.

26.5Catch your own model memorisingexploration

Use the model you train in the Chapter 27 capstone. Take 200 stories from its training set and 200 held-out stories from the same source.

  1. Compute mean log-prob and Min-K% Prob (k = 20%) for every story.
  2. For each signal, what fraction of training stories score above the median held-out story? (0.5 means no signal.)
  3. Repeat after training twice as long. Which signal grows faster?
What to expect

A small model trained for one epoch memorises little, so both signals will be weak, and Min-K% usually separates the sets somewhat better than the mean. Training longer (more epochs over the same data) strengthens both. Contamination papers run this same experiment on billion-parameter models, but here the model is small enough that you know the ground truth.

26.6Build a 150-item private eval and size it honestlythe important one

Pick a task you actually care about. Collect 150 real inputs, write a rubric of 3-5 checkable criteria, and label 50 of them independently with a colleague.

  1. Compute Cohen's κ per criterion. Rewrite any criterion with κ < 0.6 and relabel.
  2. Score two models or prompts on all 150 items and report the paired bootstrap interval of the difference.
  3. Using the measured disagreement rate, how many items would you need to detect a 3-point difference?
How to read your answer

The first rubric almost always has a criterion that people read differently ("is the tone appropriate?"). Splitting it into checks you can observe raises κ. With 150 items, expect the paired interval to be several points wide. Part 3 tells you whether to grow the set, or to accept that this eval only catches large regressions. Both are fine, as long as you know which one you have.
