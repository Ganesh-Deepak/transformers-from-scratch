12 · Numbers: fp32, fp16, bf16, fp8

Every tensor so far has been float32, and every claim assumed arithmetic is exact. Neither is true in a real training run. This chapter opens up the float — bit by bit — then shows exactly how fp16 destroys a converging model, why bf16 fixed it by giving up precision, and which handful of operations must stay in fp32 no matter what.

The one idea in this chapter

A float spends its bits on two different things: range (the exponent) and precision (the mantissa). Going from 32 bits to 16 forces you to sacrifice one of them. fp16 kept precision and lost range — and lost. bf16 kept range and threw away precision — and won. The whole chapter is the story of why that was the right trade for gradients specifically.

12.1 · What a float actually is

A floating-point number is scientific notation in binary. Three fields, packed into a fixed number of bits:

value = (−1)s × 1.mantissa × 2(exponent − bias)

The sign is one bit. The exponent decides how big the number can be — it slides the decimal point. The mantissa decides how finely you can distinguish numbers of that size. Here is 1.0 and −2.5 in all three formats, printed straight from the bits:

                sign  exponent      mantissa
  1.0   fp32     0    01111111      00000000000000000000000     (8 exp, 23 mant)
        fp16     0    01111         0000000000                  (5 exp, 10 mant)
        bf16     0    01111111      0000000                     (8 exp,  7 mant)

 -2.5   fp32     1    10000000      01000000000000000000000
        fp16     1    10000         0100000000
        bf16     1    10000000      0100000
Read those three rows again — they are the whole chapter

bf16 has the identical exponent field to fp32. Eight bits, same bias, same layout. It is literally fp32 with the bottom 16 mantissa bits deleted. fp16 has a different, smaller exponent field — 5 bits instead of 8. That single design decision is the source of every fp16 training failure in this chapter, and converting fp32 ↔ bf16 is just truncating or zero-padding, which is why it never overflows.

The two numbers that describe a format

You only need two facts about any float format. Max — the largest finite value, set by the exponent bits. And epsilon — the smallest ε where 1 + ε ≠ 1, set by the mantissa bits. Everything else follows:

>>> for dt in (torch.float32, torch.float16, torch.bfloat16):
...     fi = torch.finfo(dt); print(dt, fi.max, fi.tiny, fi.eps)

format    max            min normal      eps          decimal digits
fp32      3.40282e+38    1.17549e-38     1.19e-07     ~7.2
fp16      65504          6.10352e-05     9.77e-04     ~3.3
bf16      3.38953e+38    1.17549e-38     7.81e-03     ~2.4
fp8 e4m3  448            1.5625e-02      1.25e-01     ~1.2
fp8 e5m2  57344          6.10352e-05     2.50e-01     ~0.9

Two things should jump out. fp16's max is 65504 — not astronomically large, just sixty-five thousand. And bf16's epsilon is 8× worse than fp16's: bf16 can only distinguish numbers about 0.8% apart. Both facts matter, in opposite directions.

Verify — where the epsilon comes from
1
Add ever-smaller powers of two to 1.0 and find where they stop registering. 
for k in range(1, 14):
    if (torch.tensor(1.0, dtype=dt) + torch.tensor(2.0**-k, dtype=dt)).item() == 1.0:
        print(f"1 + 2^-{k} == 1"); break
2
The result, measured: 
fp16: 1 + 2^-11 == 1     ->  eps = 2^-10 = 0.000977
bf16: 1 +  2^-8 == 1     ->  eps =  2^-7 = 0.0078
3
Tie it back to the bit layout. fp16 has 10 stored mantissa bits, plus one implicit leading 1, giving 11 bits of significand — so the smallest distinguishable step at 1.0 is 2−10. bf16 has 7 stored + 1 implicit = 8, so 2−7. The mantissa width is the epsilon exponent. Nothing is empirical here — you could have read it off the diagram.

12.2 · The same number, both ways

Cast a handful of values through each format and the trade becomes concrete. Nothing here is a corner case; these are ordinary magnitudes that appear inside real models.

value          fp16           bf16
1              1              1
3.14159265     3.14062        3.14062        both lose the same digits here
0.1            0.0999756      0.100098       fp16 is closer
1e-8           0              1.00117e-08    <- fp16 UNDERFLOWED to zero
1e+8           inf            1.00139e+08    <- fp16 OVERFLOWED to infinity
65600          inf            65536          <- 65600 > 65504
This is not "fp16 is worse"

Look at row 3. For values fp16 can represent, it is more accurate than bf16, because it has three more mantissa bits. The failures are entirely at the extremes of the range. So the honest statement is: fp16 is the more precise format and the more fragile one. Which you want depends on whether your numbers cluster near 1 (fp16 wins) or span thirty orders of magnitude (bf16 wins). Gradients do the latter.

Measure it on something that matters

Multiply two random 512, 512 matrices in each format and compare against an fp64 reference:

max relative error vs float64, on the same 512x512 @ 512x512
  fp32    3.542e-07
  fp16    4.351e-04     <- 7.5x MORE accurate than bf16
  bf16    3.264e-03

If precision were the only thing that mattered, fp16 would have won and this chapter would be one paragraph long. The next section is why it did not.

12.3 · How fp16 kills a training run

There are two separate failures, at opposite ends of the range. Both are worth seeing directly.

Failure 1 — overflow, in the forward pass

Attention scores are a sum of d products. Their magnitude grows, and then you exponentiate them:

q, k of shape (·, 128), moderately scaled
scores = q @ k.T / sqrt(d)      ->  max score 63.85
exp(63.85)                      ->  5.367e+27   in fp32: fine
                                ->  inf         in fp16: 5.4e27 >> 65504

The fix is the one you already met in Ch 05: subtract the row maximum before exponentiating. Then the largest term is e0 = 1 and nothing can overflow — measured max after subtraction: exactly 1.0, all finite.

Now you know why that trick is mandatory rather than tidy

In fp32 the max-subtraction is a nicety; e63.85 is comfortably representable and you would never notice. In fp16 it is the difference between a number and inf. Every fused softmax kernel does it, and it is the reason FlashAttention can stream a softmax at all — its running maximum exists for exactly this reason.

The same failure appears in RMSNorm, which sums squares:

x = torch.full((4096,), 100.0)     # not a large activation
sum of squares = 4096 * 100^2 = 40,960,000

  accumulated in fp16   ->  inf        (40.9M >> 65504)
  accumulated in fp32   ->  40960000.0

Failure 2 — underflow, in the backward pass

This one is quieter, and much worse, because nothing announces it. Gradients are small. fp16's smallest representable value is 2−24 ≈ 5.96×10−8. Anything below that becomes exactly zero.

Here is that measured on this course's own model — the one from Ch 09, 623,744 parameters — by taking a real backward pass and asking what fraction of the gradients fp16 could not hold:

Measurement — the fraction of the learning signal fp16 deletes
1
At initialisation, loss 5.58. Gradients are healthy: 
median |grad|                     3.108e-04

land in the subnormal range        135,665   (21.75%)
Only 0.03% vanish. An fp16 run started here looks completely fine.
2
After 300 steps of fitting one batch, loss 0.0018. The gradients have shrunk by three orders of magnitude, because that is what convergence is: 
median |grad|                     1.008e-07
flush to ZERO in fp16              224,344   (35.97%)
subnormal (lossy) in fp16          623,611   (99.98%)
3
Read that number again. 36% of the gradients are silently replaced with zero, and 99.98% of the rest have lost precision to the subnormal range. Over a third of the model stops learning — no error, no warning, and the loss curve just flattens.
The trap: it gets worse exactly as things go well

At step 0, 0.03% of gradients vanish. At convergence, 36% do. The failure mode strengthens as the model improves, so an fp16 run can train beautifully for hours and then plateau for reasons that look like a bad learning-rate schedule. This is why "it trained fine at first" is not evidence that precision is not your problem.

The subnormal range, and why "representable" is not enough

Between 2−24 and the smallest normal value 2−14 = 6.1×10−5, fp16 uses subnormals: the implicit leading 1 is dropped, so numbers are still representable but with progressively fewer significant bits. Watch what happens to a perfectly ordinary gradient:

1e-7 stored in fp16  ->  1.19209e-07      a 19% error, and no warning

It did not underflow to zero. It is "representable". It is also wrong by a fifth. That is the 99.98% figure above: the gradients that survive are still being mangled.

12.4 · Loss scaling: the fix that made fp16 usable

The gradients are too small for fp16's range — but the range is not too narrow, it is in the wrong place. fp16 has an enormous amount of unused headroom above the gradients and up to 65504. So: move the gradients into it.

backward(S · L)  →  every gradient is S× larger  →  divide by S before the optimizer step

Because differentiation is linear, scaling the loss scales every gradient in the graph by exactly the same factor. Unscale before the optimizer sees them and the update is mathematically identical — but the intermediate values spent their life in a part of fp16's range that works.

The same converged model as above, at several scale factors:

loss scale        vanish     overflow
         1        35.97%        0.00%     <- unusable
       128         0.56%        0.00%
      1024         0.07%        0.00%
     32768         0.00%        0.00%     <- nothing lost, nothing saturated
Why the scale has to be chosen dynamically

Too small and gradients still vanish; too large and they overflow to inf. And the right value moves during training — you just saw the gradient median drop 1000× between step 0 and step 300. So real implementations use dynamic loss scaling: start high, and whenever a step produces inf or NaN, throw that step away and halve the scale; after a few hundred clean steps, double it. This is what torch.amp.GradScaler is doing, and it is why a healthy AMP run silently skips a handful of early steps.

scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")

for x in loader:
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.float16):
        _, loss, _ = model(x, targets=x)      # forward in fp16

    scaler.scale(loss).backward()             # gradients arrive S x too large
    scaler.unscale_(opt)                      # back to true scale, IN PLACE...
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)   # ...so clipping is correct
    scaler.step(opt)                          # skipped entirely if any inf/NaN
    scaler.update()                           # adjust S for next time
The bug almost everyone writes once

If you clip gradients without calling scaler.unscale_(opt) first, you are clipping the scaled gradients to norm 1.0. With S = 32768 that clips the true gradient to 3×10−5 — your model effectively stops training, and the loss curve looks like a learning-rate problem. Unscale, then clip, then step.

bf16 makes all of this disappear

bf16 has fp32's exponent field, so its smallest normal value is 1.18×10−38 rather than 6.1×10−5. Gradients at 10−7, or 10−20, are ordinary numbers. No loss scaling, no GradScaler, no skipped steps, no unscale-before-clip bug. You pay for it with 3 fewer mantissa bits — an 8× coarser epsilon.

That trade turned out to be overwhelmingly correct, and the reason is worth stating plainly: gradient descent is already a noisy, averaged process. A 0.8% error on an individual gradient is swamped by the variance of minibatch sampling. A gradient that has become exactly zero, however, is not noise — it is a permanently missing update. Range failures are structural; precision failures average out. Every large model since roughly 2020 trains in bf16 for this reason.

12.5 · What must stay in fp32

"Mixed precision" is mixed. Some things genuinely cannot be narrowed, and they are all instances of one rule: anything that accumulates, or that compares two nearly-equal numbers, needs the wider type.

The accumulator matters more than the data

Sum 4096 small positive values, varying only where the running total lives:

exact (fp64)                   20.2858076536
fp32 values, fp32 accumulate   20.2858066559     rel err 4.92e-08
fp16 values, fp16 accumulate   20.2812500000     rel err 2.25e-04
fp16 values, fp32 accumulate   20.2857170105     rel err 4.47e-06   <- 50x better
Same inputs, 50× the accuracy, from one keyword

Rows 2 and 3 hold the identical fp16 input values. The only difference is x.sum(dtype=torch.float32). The error came almost entirely from the running total, not from the data: once the sum reaches ~20, fp16's step size there is about 0.01, so every subsequent small addend is partially rounded away. This is why tensor cores multiply in fp16/bf16 but accumulate in fp32 — it is free accuracy, and it is a hardware feature, not a software choice (Ch 14).

The fp32 list

Stays fp32Why
Master weightsAn update of 10−7 added to a weight of 10−1 is invisible at bf16's 0.8% resolution — the weight simply never changes. Optimizers keep an fp32 copy and write updates there.
Adam's m and vv holds squared gradients: square 10−7 and you get 10−14, which is below fp16's floor entirely.
Norm statisticsSums of squares over d elements overflow fp16, as measured above.
Softmax internalsThe exponential sum, and the max-subtraction that keeps it finite.
The lossA scalar. Costs nothing, and it is what you make decisions from.
Matmul accumulatorsDone by the hardware; you get it for free.

This is the origin of the 16 bytes per parameter figure that dominates memory planning: bf16 weights (2) + bf16 gradients (2) + fp32 master weights (4) + fp32 m (4) + fp32 v (4). A 7B model needs 112 GB before a single activation is stored — which is the entire reason Ch 19 exists.

12.6 · fp8 and where this is going

Hopper and Blackwell added 8-bit floats. There are two, and unusually, you use both at once:

           exp  mant   max      eps      typical use
fp8 e4m3    4     3    448      0.125    weights and activations (needs precision)
fp8 e5m2    5     2    57344    0.25     gradients (needs range)

The split is this chapter's thesis stated as hardware. Forward-pass values cluster near 1 and want mantissa bits, so they get e4m3. Gradients span orders of magnitude and want exponent bits, so they get e5m2. With eps of 0.125, e4m3 resolves numbers only ~12% apart — so fp8 training needs per-tensor (or finer) scaling factors, essentially per-block loss scaling, recomputed constantly. DeepSeek-V3 trained at scale in fp8 using per-block scaling with fp32 accumulation, which is currently the clearest public demonstration that it works.

The pattern, once you see it

Every step down in width has followed the same script: the format loses range, something silently breaks, and the fix is a scale factor applied at ever-finer granularity. fp16 → one scale for the whole loss. fp8 → one per tensor or per block. Quantization to int8/int4 for inference (Ch 18) → one per group of 64 or 128 weights. It is the same idea, all the way down.

Why did bf16 replace fp16 for training, despite being the less precise format?
bf16 is faster on tensor cores
bf16 uses less memory than fp16
bf16 has fp32's exponent range, so gradients don't underflow — and precision loss averages out while a zeroed gradient does not
bf16 is more accurate for values near 1
Range beats precision for gradients. Both are 16 bits and run at the same speed. bf16 is measurably worse at representing any given number — 3.26e-3 vs 4.35e-4 relative error on a 512×512 matmul. But a gradient rounded by 0.8% still points the right way, while a gradient flushed to zero is a missing update forever. On the converged model above, fp16 zeroed 36% of them.
An fp16 run trains normally for 6 hours, then the loss plateaus. What should you suspect first?
The learning-rate schedule decayed too fast
Gradients have shrunk as the model converged and are now underflowing fp16
The data loader is repeating batches
The model has run out of capacity
The failure mode arrives with convergence. Measured on this course's model: 0.03% of gradients underflow at initialisation, 35.97% after convergence — because gradients shrink by ~1000× as loss falls. That is precisely a run that looks healthy and then stops learning. Check the loss scale, or switch to bf16.
You add gradient clipping to an fp16 + GradScaler loop and the model stops learning. Why?
Clipping is incompatible with mixed precision
The clip threshold is too high for fp16
You clipped before scaler.unscale_(), so you clipped gradients that were still S× too large — cutting the true gradient by a factor of S
GradScaler already clips, so you clipped twice
Order of operations. After scale(loss).backward() the gradients are S× inflated. Clipping their norm to 1.0 at that point means the true norm becomes 1/S — with S = 32768 the updates are ~3e-5 of what you intended. Always unscale_ → clip → step.

12.1Find your own model's underflow cliffessential

Reproduce the 36% measurement, then find where it crosses 1%. Train the tiny model on one fixed batch and, every 25 steps, record the fraction of gradients below 2−24:

FP16_MIN = 2.0 ** -24
def frac_lost(model):
    g = torch.cat([p.grad.flatten() for p in model.parameters() if p.grad is not None]).abs()
    g = g[g > 0]
    return (g < FP16_MIN).float().mean().item()

Plot frac_lost against the loss on a log-log axis. Then answer: at what loss value does fp16 start losing 1% of the signal? Is that before or after the point where you would have called the run "working"?

What you should see, and the point of it

The two curves are mirror images: as loss falls, the lost fraction climbs. The reference run went 0.03% → 35.97% while loss went 5.58 → 0.0018. The lesson is that there is no safe loss threshold you can check once — the danger zone is wherever your model is heading. This is why the fix is structural (bf16, or dynamic scaling) rather than a tuned constant.

12.2Break a softmax, then fix itcode

Write a naive softmax with no max-subtraction and run it in fp16 on scores scaled by 1, 10, and 50. Find the scale at which it first returns NaN, then add the max-subtraction and confirm all three work. Finally, explain in one sentence why the subtraction changes nothing mathematically.

def softmax_naive(x):
    e = x.exp()
    return e / e.sum(-1, keepdim=True)

def softmax_stable(x):
    e = (x - x.amax(-1, keepdim=True)).exp()
    return e / e.sum(-1, keepdim=True)
The one-sentence answer

Subtracting a constant c from every logit multiplies numerator and denominator by e−c, which cancels — so the softmax is invariant to shifts, and you are free to choose the shift that keeps the largest exponent at exactly e0 = 1. It costs one extra pass over the row, which is why FlashAttention works so hard to fuse it into the streaming loop rather than paying for it separately.

12.4Convert bf16 by handhard

Since bf16 is fp32 with the low 16 mantissa bits removed, you can convert with bit operations alone. Implement round-to-nearest-even truncation and check it against t.to(torch.bfloat16) on 100,000 random values:

def fp32_to_bf16_bits(t):
    u = t.view(torch.int32)
    # round to nearest even on bit 16, then shift
    rounding = ((u >> 16) & 1) + 0x7FFF
    return ((u + rounding) >> 16).to(torch.int16)
