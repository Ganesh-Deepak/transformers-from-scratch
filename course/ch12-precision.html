12 · Precision in Training: fp16, bf16, fp8 and the Loss Scale

Every tensor so far has been float32, and every claim assumed arithmetic is exact. Neither is true in a real training run. Chapter 0 took the formats apart bit by bit; this chapter puts them inside the loop you built in Chapter 11 and watches what breaks: exactly how fp16 destroys a converging model, why bf16 fixed it by giving up precision, which handful of values must stay in fp32 no matter what, and how the same story continues into fp8 and 4-bit training.

The one idea in this chapter

A float spends its bits on two different things: range (the exponent) and precision (the mantissa). Going from 32 bits to 16 forces you to sacrifice one of them. fp16 kept precision and lost range — and lost. bf16 kept range and threw away precision — and won. The whole chapter is the story of why that was the right trade for gradients specifically.

12.1 · The two facts from Chapter 0 that this chapter runs on

If you have not read Chapter 0, read §0.2–0.4 first; everything below assumes the bucket-and-gap picture. Two facts carry the whole chapter. The first is the bit layout of the three 16- and 32-bit training formats:

                sign  exponent      mantissa
  1.0   fp32     0    01111111      00000000000000000000000     (8 exp, 23 mant)
        fp16     0    01111         0000000000                  (5 exp, 10 mant)
        bf16     0    01111111      0000000                     (8 exp,  7 mant)

 -2.5   fp32     1    10000000      01000000000000000000000
        fp16     1    10000         0100000000
        bf16     1    10000000      0100000
Read those rows again — they are the whole chapter

bf16 has the identical exponent field to fp32. Eight bits, same bias, same layout. It is literally fp32 with the bottom 16 mantissa bits deleted. fp16 has a different, smaller exponent field — 5 bits instead of 8. That single design decision is the source of every fp16 training failure in this chapter, and converting fp32 ↔ bf16 is just rounding off or zero-padding mantissa bits. It can overflow only in the top sliver of fp32's range — measured, 3.4e38 rounds up past bf16's max of 3.3895e38 to inf — and no value a network produces comes anywhere near that.

The second is the pair of numbers each format's bits buy, which you can now derive yourself with the formulas of §0.4 — and which torch.finfo confirms:

format    max            min normal      eps          decimal digits
fp32      3.40282e+38    1.17549e-38     1.19e-07     ~7.2
fp16      65504          6.10352e-05     9.77e-04     ~3.3
bf16      3.38953e+38    1.17549e-38     7.81e-03     ~2.4
fp8 e4m3  448            1.5625e-02      1.25e-01     ~1.2
fp8 e5m2  57344          6.10352e-05     2.50e-01     ~0.9

Two things should jump out. fp16's max is 65504 — not astronomically large, just sixty-five thousand. And bf16's epsilon is 8× worse than fp16's: bf16 can only distinguish numbers about 0.8% apart. Both facts matter, in opposite directions. Keep the explorer open while you read; it starts on the three formats this chapter compares:

12.2 · The same number, both ways

Cast a handful of values through each format and the trade becomes concrete. Nothing here is a corner case; these are ordinary magnitudes that appear inside real models.

value          fp16           bf16
1              1              1
3.14159265     3.14062        3.14062        both lose the same digits here
0.1            0.0999756      0.100098       fp16 is closer
1e-8           0              1.00117e-08    <- fp16 UNDERFLOWED to zero
1e+8           inf            1.00139e+08    <- fp16 OVERFLOWED to infinity
65600          inf            65536          <- 65600 > 65504
This is not "fp16 is worse"

Look at row 3. For values fp16 can represent, it is more accurate than bf16, because it has three more mantissa bits. The failures are entirely at the extremes of the range. So the honest statement is: fp16 is the more precise format and the more fragile one. Which you want depends on whether your numbers cluster near 1 (fp16 wins) or span thirty orders of magnitude (bf16 wins). Gradients do the latter.

Measure it on something that matters

Multiply two random 512, 512 matrices in each format and compare against an fp64 reference. Inputs and output are rounded to the format and the products accumulate in fp32, which is what a tensor core does (§12.5):

max relative error vs float64, same 512x512 @ 512x512 (torch.manual_seed(0))
  fp32    3.64e-07
  fp16    4.05e-04     <- 8.8x MORE accurate than bf16
  bf16    3.58e-03

If precision were the only thing that mattered, fp16 would have won and this chapter would be one paragraph long. The next section is why it did not.

12.3 · How fp16 kills a training run

There are two separate failures, at opposite ends of the range. Both are worth seeing directly.

Failure 1 — overflow, in the forward pass

Attention scores are a sum of d products. Their magnitude grows, and then you exponentiate them:

q, k of shape (·, 128), moderately scaled
scores = q @ k.T / sqrt(d)      ->  max score 63.85
exp(63.85)                      ->  5.367e+27   in fp32: fine
                                ->  inf         in fp16: 5.4e27 >> 65504

The fix is the one you already met in Ch 05: subtract the row maximum before exponentiating. Then the largest term is e0 = 1 and nothing can overflow — measured max after subtraction: exactly 1.0, all finite.

Now you know why that trick is mandatory rather than tidy

In fp32 the max-subtraction is a nicety; e63.85 is comfortably representable and you would never notice. In fp16 it is the difference between a number and inf. Every fused softmax kernel does it, and it is the reason FlashAttention can stream a softmax at all — its running maximum exists for exactly this reason.

The same failure appears in RMSNorm, which sums squares:

x = torch.full((4096,), 100.0)     # not a large activation
sum of squares = 4096 * 100^2 = 40,960,000

  accumulated in fp16   ->  inf        (40.9M >> 65504)
  accumulated in fp32   ->  40960000.0

Failure 2 — underflow, in the backward pass

This one is quieter, and much worse, because nothing announces it. Gradients are small. fp16's smallest subnormal is 2−24 ≈ 5.96×10−8, and because casting rounds to the nearest value, anything below half of that, 2−25 ≈ 2.98×10−8, becomes exactly zero (§0.4).

Here is that measured on this course's own model — the configuration from Ch 09, 623,744 parameters — by fitting one fixed batch and, at several points, casting every gradient to fp16 and counting how many come back as zero. The script is notebooks/12_precision.ipynb; the numbers below are its output.

The trap: it gets worse exactly as things go well

At step 0, 0.01% of gradients vanish. At step 1200, 62% do. The failure mode strengthens as the model improves, so an fp16 run can train beautifully for hours and then plateau for reasons that look like a bad learning-rate schedule. This is why "it trained fine at first" is not evidence that precision is not your problem.

The subnormal range, and why "representable" is not enough

Between 2−24 and the smallest normal value 2−14 = 6.1×10−5, fp16 uses subnormals: the hidden leading 1 is dropped, so numbers are still representable but with progressively fewer significant bits. In that region the gap is a fixed 2−24, not a fraction of the value. Watch what happens to a perfectly ordinary gradient:

1e-7 stored in fp16  ->  1.19209e-07      = 2 x 2^-24, a 19% error, and no warning

It did not underflow to zero. It is "representable". It is also wrong by a fifth. That is the subnormal column above: the gradients that survive are still being mangled.

12.4 · Loss scaling: the fix that made fp16 usable

The gradients are too small for fp16's range — but the range is not too narrow, it is in the wrong place. fp16 has an enormous amount of unused headroom above the gradients and up to 65504. So: move the gradients into it.

backward(S · L)  →  every gradient is S× larger  →  divide by S before the optimizer step

Because differentiation is linear, scaling the loss scales every gradient in the graph by exactly the same factor. Unscale before the optimizer sees them and the update is mathematically identical — but the intermediate values spent their life in a part of fp16's range that works.

The same model at step 1200, its gradients multiplied by several scale factors before the fp16 cast:

loss scale        vanish     overflow
         1        62.13%        0.00%     <- unusable
       128         0.90%        0.00%
      1024         0.11%        0.00%
     32768         0.003%       0.00%     <- essentially nothing lost, nothing saturated
Why the scale has to be chosen dynamically

Too small and gradients still vanish; too large and they overflow to inf. And the right value moves during training — you just saw the gradient median drop 28,000× between step 0 and step 1200. So real implementations use dynamic loss scaling: start high, and whenever a step produces inf or NaN, throw that step away and halve the scale; after a few hundred clean steps, double it. This is what torch.amp.GradScaler is doing, and it is why a healthy AMP run silently skips a handful of early steps.

scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")

for x in loader:
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.float16):
        _, loss, _ = model(x, targets=x)      # forward in fp16

    scaler.scale(loss).backward()             # gradients arrive S x too large
    scaler.unscale_(opt)                      # back to true scale, IN PLACE...
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)   # ...so clipping is correct
    scaler.step(opt)                          # skipped entirely if any inf/NaN
    scaler.update()                           # adjust S for next time
The bug almost everyone writes once

If you clip gradients without calling scaler.unscale_(opt) first, you are clipping the scaled gradients to norm 1.0. With S = 32768 that clips the true gradient to 3×10−5 — your model effectively stops training, and the loss curve looks like a learning-rate problem. Unscale, then clip, then step.

bf16 makes all of this disappear

bf16 has fp32's exponent field, so its smallest normal value is 1.18×10−38 rather than 6.1×10−5. Gradients at 10−7, or 10−20, are ordinary numbers. No loss scaling, no GradScaler, no skipped steps, no unscale-before-clip bug. You pay for it with 3 fewer mantissa bits — an 8× coarser epsilon.

That trade turned out to be overwhelmingly correct, and the reason is worth stating plainly: gradient descent is already a noisy, averaged process. A 0.8% error on an individual gradient is swamped by the variance of minibatch sampling. A gradient that has become exactly zero, however, is not noise — it is a permanently missing update. Range failures are structural; precision failures average out. GPT-3 (2020) was still trained in fp16 with loss scaling; since about 2021–22, on hardware that supports it, nearly every large model trains in bf16 for this reason.

12.5 · What must stay in fp32

"Mixed precision" is mixed. Some things genuinely cannot be narrowed, and they are all instances of one rule: anything that accumulates, or that compares two nearly-equal numbers, needs the wider type.

The accumulator matters more than the data

Sum 4096 small positive values, uniform in [0, 0.01), varying only where the running total lives (torch.manual_seed(0)):

exact (fp64)                   20.5984571911
fp32 values, fp32 accumulate   20.5984573364     rel err 7.1e-09
fp16 values, fp16 accumulate   18.9843750000     rel err 7.8e-02   <- an fp16 running total
fp16 values, fp32 accumulate   20.5985393524     rel err 4.0e-06   <- 20,000x better
Same inputs, 20,000× the accuracy, from where the total lives

Rows 2 and 3 hold the identical fp16 input values. The only difference is the running total. Use the gap formula from §0.3: once the sum passes 16 it is in bucket [16, 32), where fp16's gap is 24−10 = 0.0156. An addend smaller than half a gap, 0.0078, rounds away entirely — and the average addend here is 0.005. So past 16 most additions simply do not happen, and the total stalls 8% short. This is why tensor cores multiply in fp16/bf16 but accumulate in fp32 — it is free accuracy, and it is a hardware feature, not a software choice (Ch 14).

A measurement trap worth knowing: x.half().sum(dtype=torch.float16) is not an fp16 accumulator. It returns 2.3e-4 relative error on this same input, because PyTorch sums at higher precision internally and rounds once at the end. Only an explicit running total — or a real fp16 accumulator in a kernel — shows the failure. An earlier version of this chapter made exactly that mistake and reported a 50× gap where the true one is about 20,000×.

The fp32 list

Stays fp32Why
Master weightsAn update of 10−7 added to a weight of 10−1 is invisible at bf16's 0.8% resolution — the weight simply never changes. Optimizers keep an fp32 copy and write updates there.
Adam's m and vv holds squared gradients: square 10−7 and you get 10−14, which is below fp16's floor entirely. And the decay itself breaks in bf16: β2 = 0.999 rounds to exactly 1.0, so v would never decay at all (§0.3, measured).
Norm statisticsSums of squares over d elements overflow fp16, as measured above.
Softmax internalsThe exponential sum, and the max-subtraction that keeps it finite.
The lossA scalar. Costs nothing, and it is what you make decisions from.
Matmul accumulatorsDone by the hardware; you get it for free.

This is the origin of the 16 bytes per parameter figure that dominates memory planning: bf16 weights (2) + bf16 gradients (2) + fp32 master weights (4) + fp32 m (4) + fp32 v (4). A 7B model needs 112 GB before a single activation is stored — which is the entire reason Ch 19 exists.

12.6 · fp8, fp4, and where this is going

Hopper, Ada and Blackwell added 8-bit floats, and Blackwell 4-bit ones. §0.5–0.6 derives every one of them; here is what changes when you put them in a training loop.

           exp  mant   max      eps      classic role
fp8 e4m3    4     3    448      0.125    weights and activations (want precision)
fp8 e5m2    5     2    57344    0.25     gradients (want range)

The original fp8 recipe — NVIDIA's Transformer Engine, after Micikevicius et al. 2022 — is this chapter's thesis stated as hardware: e4m3 forward, e5m2 backward, each tensor with its own scale factor, which is loss scaling applied per tensor and recomputed constantly. With an epsilon of 0.125, e4m3 resolves numbers only ~12% apart, so without a scale it fails immediately.

The recipes since then have moved in one direction: smaller scaling blocks. DeepSeek-V3 (2024) trained a 671B-parameter MoE in fp8 with e4m3 for every tensor, gradients included, by scaling each 1×128 tile of activations and each 128×128 block of weights separately and promoting partial sums to fp32 every 128 elements. Once each block has its own scale, it no longer needs e5m2's range — so the extra mantissa bit wins everywhere. The MX formats standardise the same idea at 32-element blocks, and NVFP4 (16-element blocks, two-level scales) has been used to pretrain a 12B model on 10T tokens with accuracy close to fp8, using stochastic rounding on the gradients (§0.8) and keeping a minority of sensitive layers in bf16.

The pattern, once you see it

Every step down in width has followed the same script: the format loses range, something silently breaks, and the fix is a scale factor applied at ever-finer granularity. fp16 → one scale for the whole loss. fp8 → one per tensor, then one per 128-element tile. fp4 → one per 16 or 32 values. Quantization to int8/int4 for inference (Ch 18) → one per group of 64 or 128 weights. And through all of it the master weights, optimizer state and accumulators stay in fp32. It is the same idea, all the way down.

Why did bf16 replace fp16 for training, despite being the less precise format?
bf16 is faster on tensor cores
bf16 uses less memory than fp16
bf16 has fp32's exponent range, so gradients don't underflow — and precision loss averages out while a zeroed gradient does not
bf16 is more accurate for values near 1
Range beats precision for gradients. Both are 16 bits and run at the same speed. bf16 is measurably worse at representing any given number — 3.58e-3 vs 4.05e-4 relative error on a 512×512 matmul. But a gradient rounded by 0.8% still points the right way, while a gradient flushed to zero is a missing update forever. On the converged model above, fp16 zeroed 62% of them.
An fp16 run trains normally for 6 hours, then the loss plateaus. What should you suspect first?
The learning-rate schedule decayed too fast
Gradients have shrunk as the model converged and are now underflowing fp16
The data loader is repeating batches
The model has run out of capacity
The failure mode arrives with convergence. Measured on this course's model: 0.01% of gradients underflow at initialisation, 62% by step 1200 — because gradients shrink by ~28,000× as loss falls. That is precisely a run that looks healthy and then stops learning. Check the loss scale, or switch to bf16.
You add gradient clipping to an fp16 + GradScaler loop and the model stops learning. Why?
Clipping is incompatible with mixed precision
The clip threshold is too high for fp16
You clipped before scaler.unscale_(), so you clipped gradients that were still S× too large — cutting the true gradient by a factor of S
GradScaler already clips, so you clipped twice
Order of operations. After scale(loss).backward() the gradients are S× inflated. Clipping their norm to 1.0 at that point means the true norm becomes 1/S — with S = 32768 the updates are ~3e-5 of what you intended. Always unscale_ → clip → step.

12.7 · Exercises

12.1Find your own model's underflow cliffessential

Reproduce the 62% measurement, then find where it crosses 1%. Train the tiny model on one fixed batch and, every 25 steps, record the fraction of gradients below 2−24:

def frac_lost(model):
    g = torch.cat([p.grad.flatten() for p in model.parameters() if p.grad is not None])
    g = g[g != 0]
    # cast for real: counting g < 2**-24 would also count values that round UP
    return (g.half() == 0).float().mean().item()

Plot frac_lost against the loss on a log-log axis. Then answer: at what loss value does fp16 start losing 1% of the signal? Is that before or after the point where you would have called the run "working"?

What you should see, and the point of it

The two curves are mirror images: as loss falls, the lost fraction climbs. The reference run went 0.01% → 62.13% while loss went 5.60 → 0.0015. The lesson is that there is no safe loss threshold you can check once — the danger zone is wherever your model is heading. This is why the fix is structural (bf16, or dynamic scaling) rather than a tuned constant.

12.2Break a softmax, then fix itcode

Write a naive softmax with no max-subtraction and run it in fp16 on scores scaled by 1, 10, and 50. Find the scale at which it first returns NaN, then add the max-subtraction and confirm all three work. Finally, explain in one sentence why the subtraction changes nothing mathematically.

def softmax_naive(x):
    e = x.exp()
    return e / e.sum(-1, keepdim=True)

def softmax_stable(x):
    e = (x - x.amax(-1, keepdim=True)).exp()
    return e / e.sum(-1, keepdim=True)
The one-sentence answer

Subtracting a constant c from every logit multiplies numerator and denominator by e−c, which cancels — so the softmax is invariant to shifts, and you are free to choose the shift that keeps the largest exponent at exactly e0 = 1. It costs one extra pass over the row, which is why FlashAttention works so hard to fuse it into the streaming loop rather than paying for it separately.

12.3Isolate the accumulatorcode

Reproduce the accumulation table in §12.5, then extend it: for n ∈ {64, 256, 1024, 4096, 16384}, plot the fp16 running total against the exact sum. You will find it does not drift smoothly — it stalls. Predict the stalling point from the gap formula before you run it: at what total does the fp16 gap reach twice the average addend?

x = torch.rand(n) * 0.01
exact = x.double().sum()
acc = torch.tensor(0., dtype=torch.float16)
for v in x.half():                   # a real fp16 running total --
    acc = acc + v                    # x.half().sum(dtype=float16) is not one (see 12.5)
same_data_fp32_acc = x.half().sum(dtype=torch.float32)
The prediction

The average addend is 0.005, so additions start vanishing once half a gap exceeds it: gap > 0.01, i.e. the bucket [16, 32) where the gap is 0.0156. Below 16 the total is roughly right; above it, it crawls. That is why the n = 4096 total stops near 19 instead of reaching 20.6.

Then look up pairwise summation, which PyTorch uses internally: adding numbers in a balanced tree keeps every partial sum close in size to what it is being added to, so the "tiny addend, huge total" situation never arises and the error grows like O(log n) instead of stalling.

12.4Convert bf16 by handhard

Since bf16 is fp32 with the low 16 mantissa bits removed, you can convert with bit operations alone. Implement round-to-nearest-even truncation and check it against t.to(torch.bfloat16) on 100,000 random values:

def fp32_to_bf16_bits(t):
    u = t.view(torch.int32)
    # round to nearest even on bit 16, then shift
    rounding = ((u >> 16) & 1) + 0x7FFF
    return ((u + rounding) >> 16).to(torch.int16)

Then answer: why does this same trick not work for fp16? Your answer should mention the exponent field specifically, and should explain why bf16 conversion overflows only above about 3.4e38 while fp16 conversion overflows above 65,520.

Finally, find the one input class where this trick is wrong: a NaN whose payload lives only in the low 16 bits, such as the pattern 0x7F800001. Rounding and shifting turns it into 0x7F80 — infinity. Real conversion routines special-case NaN first.
